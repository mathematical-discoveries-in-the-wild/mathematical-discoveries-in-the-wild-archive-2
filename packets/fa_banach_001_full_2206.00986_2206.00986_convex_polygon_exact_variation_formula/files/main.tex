\section{Source question}

Ian Doust, Michael Leinert, and Alan Stoneham define a two-dimensional
variation for functions on compact planar sets and study the associated
Banach algebras $BV(\sigma)$ and $AC(\sigma)$ \cite{DLS}.  For vertices
$\sigma=\{z_1,\ldots,z_m\}$ of a convex polygon, listed in cyclic order, their
Theorem~2.24 gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(z_i)-f(z_{i+1})|
 \leq \Var(f,\sigma),
 \qquad z_{m+1}=z_1,
 \label{eq:source-lower}
\end{equation}
together with a generally larger upper bound.  After proving equality for
three non-collinear points, they ask on PDF page~16 whether
\eqref{eq:source-lower} is always an equality when $\sigma$ has more than
three points.

\section{Definitions}

Let $S=[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ be a finite ordered list of elements of $\sigma$.
The source defines
\[
 \cvar(f,S)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f(x_j)-f(x_{j-1})|.
\]
Its variation factor $\vf(S)$ is the largest, over all lines $L$ in the
plane, of the number of connected components of the intersection of $L$ with
the polygonal path through $S$.  Equivalently, it is the maximum number of
crossing segments under the source's endpoint convention.  Then
\[
 \Var(f,\sigma)=\sup_S\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}.
\]
For later use, put
\begin{equation}
 \Per(f)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(z_i)-f(z_{i+1})|,
 \qquad z_{m+1}=z_1.
 \label{eq:halfperimeter}
\end{equation}
Thus the question is whether $\Var(f,\sigma)=\Per(f)$.

\section{Proof intuition}

For a real-valued function, split every absolute difference into its level
sets.  At a fixed level, the vertices above that level form several runs
around the polygon.  Convexity lets one cut off each run by a line.  Every
entry into or exit from that run is therefore a crossing of that line, so one
run contributes at most $\vf(S)$ transitions to any list.  Integrating over
the levels gives exactly half the cyclic variation of the vertex values.

This proves the desired upper bound first for real functions, with no
monotonicity assumption.  A complex distance is the average absolute length
of its real projections.  Averaging the real theorem over all projection
directions gives the full complex-valued result, while the source already
provides the reverse inequality.

\section{The cyclic-run bound}

A \emph{cyclic interval} is a nonempty proper set of consecutive indices in
$\{1,\ldots,m\}$, with indices read modulo $m$.

\begin{lemma}[Chord separation]
\label{lem:separator}
For every cyclic interval $I$, there is a line $L_I$ which strictly separates
$\{z_i:i\in I\}$ from $\{z_i:i\notin I\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
There are exactly two polygon edges at which the boundary arc indexed by $I$
meets its complementary arc.  Choose one point in the relative interior of
each of these two edges and let $L_I$ be the line through the chosen points.
Convexity implies that the intersection of the polygon with $L_I$ is precisely
the chord between those points.  The two open boundary arcs lie in the two
opposite open half-planes.  Since the chord endpoints were chosen inside the
edges, no vertex lies on $L_I$, giving strict separation.
\end{proof}

For $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$ and a list $S=[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$, let
$N_A(S)$ be the number of $j$ for which exactly one of $x_{j-1},x_j$ belongs
to $\{z_i:i\in A\}$.

\begin{lemma}[Run-transition inequality]
\label{lem:runs}
If $A$ has $k$ cyclic runs, then
\begin{equation}
 N_A(S)\leq k\,\vf(S).
 \label{eq:run-bound}
\end{equation}
Here the two trivial sets $A=\varnothing$ and
$A=\{1,\ldots,m\}$ have $k=0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every transition counted by $N_A(S)$ has a unique endpoint in $A$; assign the
transition to the cyclic run containing that endpoint.  For one fixed run
$I$, Lemma~\ref{lem:separator} supplies a line $L_I$ strictly separating its
vertices from all other vertices.  Both endpoints of every transition
assigned to $I$ lie on strictly opposite sides of $L_I$.  Its list segment is
therefore a type-(1) crossing segment on $L_I$.  The number assigned to $I$
is at most
\[
 \vf(S,L_I)\leq\vf(S).
\]
Summing over the $k$ runs gives \eqref{eq:run-bound}.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact formula}

\begin{lemma}[Real-valued case]
\label{lem:real}
If $g:\sigma\to\R$, then $\Var(g,\sigma)\leq\Per(g)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $t\in\R$, set
\[
 A_t=\{i:g(z_i)>t\},
\]
and let $k_t$ be its number of cyclic runs.  The layer-cake identity
\[
 |a-b|=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
 \big|\mathbf1_{\{a>t\}}-\mathbf1_{\{b>t\}}\big|\,dt
\]
and finiteness of the list give
\begin{equation}
 \cvar(g,S)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}N_{A_t}(S)\,dt.
 \label{eq:list-layercake}
\end{equation}
Around the cyclically ordered vertices, the indicator of a nontrivial $A_t$
changes value exactly twice per run.  Applying layer cake to the polygon
edges therefore gives
\begin{align}
 \Per(g)
 &=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}|g(z_i)-g(z_{i+1})|\notag\\
 &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_t\,dt.
 \label{eq:per-layercake}
\end{align}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:runs}, equations \eqref{eq:list-layercake} and
\eqref{eq:per-layercake} yield
\[
 \cvar(g,S)\leq\vf(S)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_t\,dt
 =\vf(S)\Per(g).
\]
Divide by $\vf(S)$ and take the supremum over all lists $S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite-convex variation]
\label{thm:main}
Let $\sigma=\{z_1,\ldots,z_m\}$ be the vertices of a convex polygon, listed
in cyclic order.  For every $f:\sigma\to\C$,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\displaystyle
 \Var(f,\sigma)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(z_i)-f(z_{i+1})|},
 \qquad z_{m+1}=z_1.
 \label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}
In particular, the lower bound in the source's equation~(2.1) is always exact,
including when $m>3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$, define the real-valued projection
\[
 g_\theta(z_i)=\Re\bigl(e^{-i\theta}f(z_i)\bigr).
\]
The elementary Cauchy projection identity
\begin{equation}
 |w|=\frac12\int_0^\pi
 \big|\Re(e^{-i\theta}w)\big|\,d\theta,
 \qquad w\in\C,
 \label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
follows by rotation from $\int_0^\pi|\cos\theta|\,d\theta=2$.
For any list $S$, finite summation and \eqref{eq:projection} give
\[
 \cvar(f,S)=\frac12\int_0^\pi\cvar(g_\theta,S)\,d\theta.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:real}, applied before taking the list supremum, says
$\cvar(g_\theta,S)\leq\vf(S)\Per(g_\theta)$.  Hence
\begin{align*}
 \cvar(f,S)
 &\leq \frac{\vf(S)}2\int_0^\pi\Per(g_\theta)\,d\theta\\
 &=\vf(S)\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(z_i)-f(z_{i+1})|\\
 &=\vf(S)\Per(f),
\end{align*}
where the middle equality is another application of
\eqref{eq:projection}.  Thus $\Var(f,\sigma)\leq\Per(f)$.  The reverse
inequality is precisely the source's lower bound
\eqref{eq:source-lower}, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Finite norm formula]
For every $f:\sigma\to\C$,
\[
 \lVert f\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}
 =\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|f(z_i)|
 +\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(z_i)-f(z_{i+1})|.
\]
Thus the norm is finitely computable and depends on the polygon only through
the cyclic order of its vertices.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Substitute Theorem~\ref{thm:main} into the source definition
$\lVert f\rVert_{BV}=\lVert f\rVert_\infty+\Var(f,\sigma)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification and scope}

The included script \texttt{code/verify\_cyclic\_cut.py} exhaustively checks
all non-backtracking four-vertex words through length nine against all
$\{-1,0,1\}$-valued functions.  It also tests 20,000 deterministic random
complex instances for four through eight vertices.  It verifies the stronger
combinatorial inequality using the largest cyclic-interval transition count,
which Lemma~\ref{lem:separator} bounds above by $\vf(S)$.  The computation is
only a stress test; the proof is analytic.  Commands and outputs are recorded
in \texttt{verification.md}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} answers the finite-convex-set question in its full
complex-valued scope.  It does not address arbitrary finite planar sets with
interior points or nonconvex position, nor the separate extension and lattice
questions later in the source.

\section{Human-review recommendation}

This packet is suitable for expert review as a candidate full solution.  The
highest-value check is Lemma~\ref{lem:separator}, especially the cases of a
single-vertex run and an all-but-one-vertex run.  The chord endpoints lie in

type-(1) crossing convention apply.  The remaining layer-cake and projection
steps use only finite sums and elementary integrals.
