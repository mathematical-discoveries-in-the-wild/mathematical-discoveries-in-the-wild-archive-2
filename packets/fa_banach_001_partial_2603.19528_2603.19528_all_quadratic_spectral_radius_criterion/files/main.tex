\section{Source question and setup}

Let $c_1,c_2$ be freely independent circular operators and
\[
 P(c_1,c_2)=\sum_{i,j=1}^2 a_{ij}c_ic_j+\sum_{i=1}^2 b_i c_i,
 \qquad A=(a_{ij})\in M_2(\C),\quad b=(b_1,b_2)^t\in\C^2.
\]
For $\lambda\ne0$, set $A_\lambda=\lambda^{-1}A$ and
$b_\lambda=\lambda^{-1}b$.  Miyagawa's quadratic-spectrum theorem
\cite[Theorem~1.4 and Theorem~4.6]{Miyagawa} defines
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
 Q_\lambda=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 b_\lambda^*b_\lambda&\tr(A_\lambda^*A_\lambda)&
 b_\lambda^*A_\lambda&b_\lambda^t\overline{A_\lambda}\\
 1&0&0&0\\
 \overline{b_\lambda}&0&0&\overline{A_\lambda}\\
 b_\lambda&0&A_\lambda&0
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad e_1=(1,0,0,0,0,0)^t,
\end{equation}
where the block sizes are $1,1,2,2$, and proves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source-spectrum}
 \lambda\in\specop(P(c_1,c_2))
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \lambda=0\ \text{or}\ Q_\lambda^n e_1\not\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

The source observes that the right side of \eqref{eq:source-spectrum} implies
$r(Q_\lambda)\ge1$, but leaves open whether the converse always holds.  It
proves the converse under any of the extra assumptions $b=0$,
$\overline A A$ has no distinct real eigenvalues, or $A$ is symmetric
\cite[Remark~1.5 and Remark~4.7]{Miyagawa}.

\section{Proof intuition}

Suppose a dominant eigenvalue of $Q_\lambda$ were invisible from the orbit of
$e_1$.  A left eigenvector calculation forces it into the lower $4\times4$
block
\[
 B=\begin{pmatrix}0&\overline{A_\lambda}\\A_\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The determinant of $Q_\lambda$ is so large relative to a hypothetical complex
or smaller-modulus eigenvalue of $\overline{A_\lambda}A_\lambda$ that only one
case survives: the square of the hidden eigenvalue is the larger of two
distinct nonnegative eigenvalues.

In that case a unit eigenvector $x$ is automatically a con-eigenvector:
$A_\lambda x=\overline\alpha\,\overline x$, with
$|\alpha|=r(Q_\lambda)$.  A root of the scalar equation
\[
 \overline\alpha t^2+(b_\lambda^t x)t=1
\]
has modulus at most $|\alpha|^{-1/2}\le1$.  Thus $z=tx$ is in the closed unit
ball and $P(z)=\lambda$.  Scalar values of a free polynomial on that ball lie
in the spectrum of its left-creation realization by the free Szeg\H{o} kernel.
The source proves that this spectrum equals that of $P(c_1,c_2)$, contradicting
the assumed invisibility through \eqref{eq:source-spectrum}.

\section{Two lemmas}

Write $L_1,L_2$ for the left creation operators on the full Fock space over
$\C^2$.  For a scalar pair $z=(z_1,z_2)$, $P(z)$ means the ordinary scalar
evaluation of the same noncommutative polynomial.

\begin{lemma}[scalar evaluations are spectral]\label{lem:scalar}
If $\|z\|_2\le1$, then $P(z)\in\specop(P(L_1,L_2))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First suppose $\|z\|_2<1$.  For a word $w=w_1\cdots w_n$, put
$z_w=z_{w_1}\cdots z_{w_n}$ and define the free Szeg\H{o} vector
\[
 k_z=\sum_w\overline{z_w}\,e_w.
\]
It is nonzero and belongs to Fock space because
$\|k_z\|^2=\sum_{n\ge0}\|z\|_2^{2n}<\infty$.  Moreover
$L_i^*k_z=\overline{z_i}k_z$, and hence
\[
 P(L_1,L_2)^*k_z=\overline{P(z)}k_z.
\]
Thus $P(z)$ is in the spectrum.  If $\|z\|_2=1$, apply the strict-ball result
to $rz$ for $0<r<1$ and let $r\uparrow1$.  Since spectra are closed and
$P(rz)\to P(z)$, the boundary case follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[form of an invisible dominant eigenvalue]\label{lem:hidden}
Let $C\in M_2(\C)$ and $d\in\C^2$, and let $Q$ be the matrix in
\eqref{eq:Q} with $(A_\lambda,b_\lambda)$ replaced by $(C,d)$.  Suppose
$Q^ne_1\to0$ and $r(Q)\ge1$.  Then there are a unit vector $x\in\C^2$ and
$\alpha\in\C$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:con}
 \overline C\,\overline x=\alpha x,
 \qquad Cx=\overline\alpha\,\overline x,
 \qquad |\alpha|=r(Q).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose an eigenvalue $\rho$ of $Q$ with $|\rho|=r(Q)$ and a nonzero left
eigenvector $v=(v_1,\ldots,v_6)$.  Since
\[
 vQ^ne_1=\rho^n v_1\longrightarrow0
\]
and $|\rho|\ge1$, one has $v_1=0$.  The second coordinate of $vQ=\rho v$
then gives $v_2=0$.  Consequently $(v_3,\ldots,v_6)$ is a nonzero left
eigenvector, with eigenvalue $\rho$, of
\[
 B=\begin{pmatrix}0&\overline C\\C&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Thus $\rho^2$ is an eigenvalue of $M=\overline C C$.

Let $\theta_1,\theta_2$ be the eigenvalues of $M$, ordered so that
$|\theta_1|\ge|\theta_2|$.  Their sum is real and their product is
$|\det C|^2\ge0$, so they are either a nonreal conjugate pair or real numbers
of the same sign.  Expanding $\det Q$ first along its second row and then along
the remaining scalar column gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det}
 |\det Q|=T|\det C|^2,
 \qquad T=\tr(C^*C).
\end{equation}
As $|\rho|$ is the largest eigenvalue modulus of the $6\times6$ matrix $Q$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det-upper}
                     |\det Q|\le|\rho|^6.
\end{equation}

If $\theta_1,\theta_2$ are a nonreal conjugate pair, or if they are equal and
nonzero, then $|\rho|^2=|\det C|$.  The singular-value inequality
$T\ge2|\det C|$ makes \eqref{eq:det} at least
$2|\det C|^3$, contradicting \eqref{eq:det-upper}.  The zero repeated-root
case would give $\rho=0$.  Hence the roots are distinct and real.

If $\rho^2=\theta_2$, then $\theta_2\ne0$ and
$T\ge r(M)=|\theta_1|$.  Equations \eqref{eq:det}--\eqref{eq:det-upper} give
\[
 |\theta_1|^2|\theta_2|
 \le T|\theta_1\theta_2|
 =|\det Q|
 \le|\theta_2|^3,
\]
which is impossible because $|\theta_1|>|\theta_2|$.  Therefore
$\rho^2=\theta_1$.

Let $x$ be a unit eigenvector of $M$ for the simple real eigenvalue
$\theta_1$.  Conjugating $Mx=\theta_1x$ shows that
$\overline C\,\overline x$ is another eigenvector for $\theta_1$; it is
nonzero because $\theta_1=\rho^2\ne0$.  Simplicity gives
$\overline C\,\overline x=\alpha x$ for some $\alpha\ne0$.  Conjugation gives
$Cx=\overline\alpha\,\overline x$, and substitution into $Mx=\theta_1x$
gives $\theta_1=|\alpha|^2>0$.  Finally
$|\alpha|=|\rho|=r(Q)$, proving \eqref{eq:con}.
\end{proof}

\section{The all-coefficient criterion}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every $A\in M_2(\C)$, $b\in\C^2$, and $\lambda\ne0$,
\[
 Q_\lambda^ne_1\not\longrightarrow0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 r(Q_\lambda)\ge1.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \specop(P(c_1,c_2))
 =\{0\}\cup\{\lambda\in\C\setminus\{0\}:r(Q_\lambda)\ge1\}.
\]
No symmetry, eigenvalue, or vanishing-linear-term hypothesis is required.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $Q_\lambda^ne_1$ does not tend to zero, the Jordan normal form immediately
gives $r(Q_\lambda)\ge1$.

Conversely, suppose $r(Q_\lambda)\ge1$ but
$Q_\lambda^ne_1\to0$.  Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:hidden} with
$C=A_\lambda$ and $d=b_\lambda$, obtaining $x$ and $\alpha$.  Since $x$ is
unit,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bilinear}
                   x^tCx=\overline\alpha.
\end{equation}
Put $\beta=d^t x$.  The two roots of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root}
             \overline\alpha t^2+\beta t-1=0
\end{equation}
have product $-1/\overline\alpha$.  Hence at least one root satisfies
\[
 |t|\le|\alpha|^{-1/2}=r(Q_\lambda)^{-1/2}\le1.
\]
For $z=tx$, equations \eqref{eq:bilinear} and \eqref{eq:root} yield
\[
 z^tA_\lambda z+b_\lambda^tz=1,
 \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P(z)=\lambda.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar} therefore places $\lambda$ in
$\specop(P(L_1,L_2))$.  Miyagawa proves
$\specop(P(L_1,L_2))=\specop(P(c_1,c_2))$
\cite[Corollary after Lemma~4.1]{Miyagawa}.  Now
\eqref{eq:source-spectrum} implies
$Q_\lambda^ne_1\not\to0$, a contradiction.  This proves the equivalence, and
the displayed spectral formula follows from \eqref{eq:source-spectrum}.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification, scope, and novelty audit}

The proof is exact and has no computational dependency.  The determinant
identity \eqref{eq:det} can be checked directly by the two indicated Laplace
expansions; the remaining determinant estimates use only
$r(\overline C C)\le\|C\|^2\le\tr(C^*C)$ and
$\tr(C^*C)\ge2|\det C|$.  The scalar-evaluation lemma includes the boundary
of the ball by radial approximation, so no nonnormal approximate-eigenvector
claim is being assumed.

The theorem fully answers the sharper question in Remarks~1.5 and 4.7 and
removes all three restrictions listed there.  It does not settle the broader
question in the introduction asking whether the spectrum of every rational or
polynomial function in free semicircular operators equals the support of its
Brown measure.  In particular, weak convergence of quadratic Ginibre
eigenvalue measures identifies the limiting Brown measure but does not by
itself exclude spectral regions carrying zero Brown mass; proving support
equality would require additional lower-density or Brown-potential control.

A bounded novelty audit searched the run's result, attempt, and proof-gap
indexes, arXiv:2603.19528, the exact title and spectral-radius phrasing, and the
source's cited quadratic-Ginibre reference.  No later theorem resolving the
all-coefficient criterion was located.  Because the source is very recent and
the final external-source refresh was unavailable, novelty is plausible but
remains pending specialist review.

Recommended human-review focus is: (1) the block-order and transpose
conventions in Lemma~\ref{lem:hidden}; (2) the determinant comparison excluding
all cases except the positive simple eigenvalue; and (3) the use of the source's
spectrum equality and recursion theorem in the last contradiction.
