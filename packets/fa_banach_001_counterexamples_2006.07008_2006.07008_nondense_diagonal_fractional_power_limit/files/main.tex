\begin{abstract}
Batty and Chen ask whether their Komatsu--Chen domain-characterization limit
holds for the missing exponents $\alpha=n+it$.  On $\Lin$, let $A$ be the
diagonal operator with eigenvalues $k$.  For $x_k=k^{-\alpha}$ and
$\beta=\alpha+1$, one has $A^\alpha x=\one$, but the normalized truncation at
$N$ is exactly $((N/(N+k))^\alpha)_k$.  These truncations lie in the closed
subspace $c_0$ and converge coordinatewise to $\one\notin c_0$, so they do not
converge in $\Lin$.  In fact, the same counterexample works for every
$\RePart\alpha>0$.
\end{abstract}

On source PDF p.~16, Lemma 2.10 asserts the converse representation except
possibly when $\RePart\alpha\in\N$ and $\ImPart\alpha\ne0$, and Remark 2.11 asks
whether it remains true for $\alpha=n+it$:

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/open_problem_crop.png}

Thus, with $\alpha,\beta\in\C_+$ and
$\RePart\alpha<\RePart\beta$, the question is whether every
$x\in D(A^\alpha)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
 A^\alpha x=
 \lim_{N\to\infty}
 \frac{\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta-\alpha)}
 \int_0^N \lambda^\alpha A^\beta(\lambda+A)^{-\beta}x\,
 \frac{d\lambda}{\lambda}
\end{equation}
in the case $\alpha=n+it$, $n\in\N$, $t\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$.

The nondense-domain scope is essential here.  The paper says explicitly in
its introduction that its general non-negative operators need not be densely
defined or have dense range (source PDF p.~2):

\section{Proof intuition}

For a nondensely defined operator, fractional powers can map a vector in their
domain to a point outside $\overline{D(A)}$.  In contrast, every truncated
resolvent integral in \eqref{eq:target} remains inside
$\overline{D(A)}$.  A diagonal operator on $\Lin$ makes this mismatch exact:
$\overline{D(A)}=c_0$, while a carefully chosen $x$ has
$A^\alpha x=\one$.  Taking $\beta=\alpha+1$ collapses the incomplete beta
integral to the elementary antiderivative
$(u/(1+u))^\alpha$, so there are no approximation or functional-calculus
ambiguities.

\section{Counterexample}

We use principal complex powers of positive real numbers.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
Let $\alpha\in\C$ satisfy $\RePart\alpha>0$ and set
$\beta=\alpha+1$.  There are a complex Banach space $X$, a positive operator
$A$ on $X$, and $x\in D(A^\alpha)$ for which the limit in
\eqref{eq:target} does not exist.  Consequently, Remark 2.11 has a negative
answer in the generality stated in the source paper.  In particular this
applies to every $\alpha=n+it$ with $n\in\N$ and $t\ne0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take $X=\Lin(\N)$ and define
\[
 D(A)=\{z=(z_k)_{k\ge1}\in\Lin:(kz_k)_{k\ge1}\in\Lin\},
 \qquad (Az)_k=kz_k.
\]

\emph{Operator hypotheses.}
The operator is closed: if $z^{(j)}\to z$ and $Az^{(j)}\to w$ in
$\Lin$, then coordinate evaluation gives $w_k=kz_k$, whence
$(kz_k)=w\in\Lin$.  For $\lambda>0$,
\[
 ((\lambda+A)^{-1}z)_k=\frac{z_k}{\lambda+k},
 \qquad
 \|\lambda(\lambda+A)^{-1}\|
 =\sup_{k\ge1}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+k}\le1.
\]
This resolvent maps $X$ onto $D(A)$.  Moreover $0\in\rho(A)$ and
$A^{-1}$ is multiplication by $k^{-1}$.  Thus $A$ is positive, hence
non-negative, in the terminology of the source.  Also
\[
 D(A)\subset c_0,
 \qquad c_{00}\subset D(A),
 \qquad \overline{D(A)}=c_0.
\]

\emph{Fractional powers.}
Put $B=A^{-1}$.  The bounded operator $B$ is diagonal with entries $k^{-1}$.
The Balakrishnan--Komatsu formula for a bounded non-negative operator, applied
coordinatewise, gives
\[
 (B^\alpha z)_k=k^{-\alpha}z_k.
\]
Indeed, for any integer $m>\RePart\alpha$, its scalar integral is the usual
beta integral
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(m)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(m-\alpha)}
 \int_0^\infty \lambda^{\alpha-1}
 \frac{k^{-m}}{(\lambda+k^{-1})^m}\,d\lambda=k^{-\alpha},
\]
and the operator-valued integral converges in norm.  By the source's
definition for a positive unbounded operator,
$A^\alpha=(B^\alpha)^{-1}$.  Hence
\[
 D(A^\alpha)=\{z\in\Lin:(k^\alpha z_k)_k\in\Lin\},
 \qquad (A^\alpha z)_k=k^\alpha z_k.
\]

\emph{The witness and the exact truncation.}
Let $x_k=k^{-\alpha}$.  Since $\RePart\alpha>0$, $x\in c_0\subset X$;
the preceding formula shows that $x\in D(A^\alpha)$ and
$A^\alpha x=\one$.  The same diagonal functional-calculus computation gives
$((\lambda+A)^{-\beta}z)_k=(\lambda+k)^{-\beta}z_k$ and
$(A^\beta z)_k=k^\beta z_k$ on its domain.  Since
$\beta=\alpha+1$,
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta-\alpha)}=\alpha.
\]
The $k$th coordinate of the normalized truncation $T_Nx$ in
\eqref{eq:target} is therefore
\begin{align*}
 (T_Nx)_k
 &=\alpha\int_0^N
   \lambda^{\alpha-1}k(\lambda+k)^{-\alpha-1}\,d\lambda\\
 &=\alpha\int_0^{N/k}u^{\alpha-1}(1+u)^{-\alpha-1}\,du\\
 &=\left(\frac{N}{N+k}\right)^\alpha,
\end{align*}
because
\[
 \frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{u}{1+u}\right)^\alpha
 =\alpha u^{\alpha-1}(1+u)^{-\alpha-1}.
\]
The finite-cutoff Bochner integral is legitimate: after division by
$\lambda$, its norm is $O(\lambda^{\RePart\alpha-1})$ near zero and the
integrand is norm continuous away from zero.

\emph{Nonconvergence.}
For each finite $N$, $(T_Nx)_k\to0$ as $k\to\infty$, so $T_Nx\in c_0$.
For each fixed $k$, however, $(T_Nx)_k\to1$ as $N\to\infty$.  If $(T_Nx)$
converged in $\Lin$, the continuity of every coordinate evaluation would
force its limit to be $\one$.  But $c_0$ is norm closed and every $T_Nx$ lies
in $c_0$, whereas $\one\notin c_0$.  This contradiction proves that the
limit does not exist.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification, scope, and limitations}

The proof is entirely explicit.  The main adversarial checks are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item $A:D(A)\to X$ is bijective although $D(A)$ is not dense; its inverse is
bounded on all of $X$, exactly as required by $0\in\rho(A)$.
\item The fractional-power computation follows from the same bounded-inverse
definition used in the source, not from an unproved coordinatewise convention.
\item For every finite $N$, the integral is a genuine Bochner integral in
$\Lin$; only the improper limit fails.
\item The contradiction is norm-topological, not merely the observation that
pointwise convergence need not be uniform.
\end{enumerate}

The example actually disproves the analogue of Lemma 2.10 for every
$\alpha\in\C_+$ if nondense domains are admitted.  It does not address the
variant under the additional hypothesis $\overline{D(A)}=X$.  The 2016
Chen--Li paper cited by Batty--Chen is described as treating densely defined
operators; no contradiction with that restricted theorem is claimed.

An optional script, \texttt{code/verifier.py}, compares numerical quadrature
with the exact closed form for a sample nonreal exponent.  This is only a
sanity check; the antiderivative above is the proof.

\section{Bounded literature and novelty check}

Searches on 11 August 2026 covered the run's registry, solution, attempt, and
proof-gap indexes; the exact arXiv id and title; the phrases ``mystery of
imaginary powers,'' ``$n+it$,'' and ``characterizations of domains of
fractional powers''; the cited Chen--Li paper; title/citation variants; and
combinations of non-densely defined fractional powers with diagonal operators
on $\Lin$ and $c_0$.  Background literature on fractional powers of
non-densely defined operators was found, but no later paper explicitly
answering Remark 2.11 and no matching diagonal counterexample was located.
Novelty is therefore provisional.

\section{Human-review recommendation}

Verify first that Remark 2.11 is intended in the paper's advertised general
framework, rather than with an unstated dense-domain restriction.  Then check
the inverse-based fractional-power domain and the one-line antiderivative.  If
density was silently intended, reclassify the result as a sharp obstruction:
it proves that density is indispensable, while leaving the dense-domain
question open.
