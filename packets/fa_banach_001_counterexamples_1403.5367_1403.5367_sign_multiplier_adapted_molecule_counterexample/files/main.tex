\noindent\textbf{Status.}
Candidate full negative answer to the concrete uncertainty in Remark~7.3,
likely valid pending expert review.

\section{Source question}

Auscher and Stahlhut work with a constant-coefficient first-order operator
$D$, an accretive multiplication operator $B$, and Hardy spaces associated
with the bisectorial operators $DB$ and $BD$.  In their perturbation argument,
an adapted atom $a=Du$ is sent by a bounded functional-calculus multiplier to
\[
 m=b(DB)a=Dv.
\]
Their estimates show that $m$ is a classical Hardy-space molecule, but the
available bound for its potential $v$ loses an extra annular factor $2^j$.
Remark~7.3 asks whether $m$ is nevertheless a single
$(\mathbb H_D^p,\varepsilon,1)$-molecule in their adapted sense
\cite{AuscherStahlhut}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/open_problem_crop.png}
  \caption{Remark 7.3 on source PDF page 45.}
\end{figure}

For the case used below, $n=p=1$, the relevant part of the definition says
that a normalized $(\mathbb H_D^1,\varepsilon,1)$-molecule $m$ associated
with an interval $I$ of length $\ell(I)$ has a potential $v\in\operatorname{Dom}D$
such that $Dv=m$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:molecule}
 \left\|(\ell(I)D)^{-1}m\right\|_{L^2(S_j(I))}
 \le (2^j\ell(I))^{-1/2}2^{-j\varepsilon}
 \qquad(j\ge0),
\end{equation}
where $S_j(I)=2^jI\setminus2^{j-1}I$ for $j\ge1$.  Allowing a fixed
multiplicative normalization constant would make no difference below.

\section{Proof intuition}

The multiplier that most directly exposes the obstruction is the sign of the
derivative.  On the Fourier side, $D=-i\,d/dx$ has symbol $\xi$, while
$\operatorname{sgn}(D)$ is, up to a constant, the Hilbert transform.  Applying
it to $a=Du$ gives a potential
\[
 v=\operatorname{sgn}(D)u.
\]
If the compactly supported $u$ has nonzero integral, the Hilbert transform has
the unavoidable far-field term $c/x$.  Its $L^2$ mass on an annulus of radius
$R$ is therefore comparable from below to $R^{-1/2}$.  The positive
$\varepsilon$ in the adapted molecule definition demands the strictly faster
rate $R^{-1/2-\varepsilon}$.  Since $D$ has no nonzero $L^2$ kernel, one cannot
choose a different potential to remove this tail.

\section{Counterexample}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
In the admissible model
\[
 n=N=1,\qquad D=-i\frac{d}{dx}\text{ on }L^2(\R),\qquad B=I,
\]
there are an $(\mathbb H_D^1,1)$-atom $a$ and a function
$\beta\in\Hinf(S_\mu)$ such that $m=\beta(DB)a$ is not an
$(\mathbb H_D^1,\varepsilon,1)$-molecule for any $\varepsilon>0$.
Indeed, $m$ is not such a molecule relative to any interval.  Consequently,
the question in Remark~7.3 has a negative answer.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $Q=(-1,1)$.  Choose $\varphi\in C_c^\infty((-1/2,1/2))$ with
\[
 M_0:=\int_\R\varphi(y)\,dy\ne0.
\]
For a sufficiently small nonzero scalar $c$, put $u=c\varphi$ and $a=Du$.
Then $a$ and $u$ are supported in $Q$, and scaling $c$ makes
\[
 \|a\|_2\le |Q|^{-1/2},
 \qquad
 \|u\|_2\le \ell(Q)|Q|^{-1/2}.
\]
These are precisely the two normalization estimates for an
$(\mathbb H_D^1,1)$-atom in the source's definition.  Notice that
$\int u=cM_0\ne0$.

The bisector $S_\mu$ has two connected components.  Define
\[
 \beta(z)=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&z\in S_{\mu,+},\\
 -1,&z\in S_{\mu,-}.
 \end{cases}
\]
This is a bounded holomorphic function on $S_\mu$, with
$\|\beta\|_\infty=1$.  The Fourier symbol of $D$ is $\xi$, so
$\beta(D)$ is the multiplier $\operatorname{sgn}\xi$.  Set
\[
 m=\beta(D)a.
\]
Functional calculus commutes with $D$ on its domain; therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:potential}
 m=\beta(D)Du=D\beta(D)u.
\end{equation}
Put $v=\beta(D)u$.  Since the $L^2$ kernel of $D$ is zero, equation
\eqref{eq:potential} also shows that $v$ is the \emph{unique} $L^2$ potential
of $m$.

With the convention
\[
 Hf(x)=\frac1\pi\operatorname{p.v.}\int_\R\frac{f(y)}{x-y}\,dy,
\]
the Hilbert transform has Fourier multiplier $-i\operatorname{sgn}\xi$.
Thus $v=iHu$.  For $|x|$ larger than twice the support radius of $u$, no
principal value is needed and
\begin{align}\label{eq:asymptotic}
 v(x)
 &=\frac{i}{\pi}\int_\R\frac{u(y)}{x-y}\,dy\\
 &=\frac{i}{\pi x}\int_\R u(y)\,dy+O(|x|^{-2}).
\end{align}
The coefficient of $x^{-1}$ is nonzero.  Hence there are $R_0,c_0>0$ such
that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pointlower}
 |v(x)|\ge \frac{c_0}{|x|}\qquad(x\ge R_0).
\end{equation}

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $m$ is an
$(\mathbb H_D^1,\varepsilon,1)$-molecule for some $\varepsilon>0$, associated
with some fixed interval $I$.  By uniqueness, the potential occurring in its
definition must be the above $v$.  For all sufficiently large $j$, the right
component of $S_j(I)$ contains an interval of length comparable to
$R_j=2^j\ell(I)$ on which $x$ is comparable to $R_j$.  From
\eqref{eq:pointlower},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower}
 \|v\|_{L^2(S_j(I))}\ge c_I R_j^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, multiplying \eqref{eq:molecule} by $\ell(I)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper}
 \|v\|_{L^2(S_j(I))}
 \le \ell(I)R_j^{-1/2}2^{-j\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
The ratio of the lower bound in \eqref{eq:lower} to the upper bound in
\eqref{eq:upper} grows like $2^{j\varepsilon}$.  This is impossible as
$j\to\infty$.  Since both $I$ and $\varepsilon>0$ were arbitrary, $m$ is not
a first-order adapted molecule relative to any interval.
\end{proof}

\section{What the example shows}

The failure is specifically in the potential estimate ($k=1$), not in the
ordinary decay of $m$.  Differentiating \eqref{eq:asymptotic} gives a
$|x|^{-2}$ tail for $m=Dv$, consistent with the source's conclusion that
$m$ is a classical $H^1$ molecule.  The primitive necessarily loses one
power, exactly the low-frequency phenomenon reflected by the source's extra
$2^j$ factor.  Thus that loss cannot be removed in the stated generality.

The counterexample does not address the memoir's broader questions about
endpoint functional calculus, abstract atomic decompositions, or stability at
all coincidence exponents.

\section{Verification and novelty status}

The proof was checked against the source definitions at four points: the
normalization of an adapted atom; admissibility of a function taking separate
constants on the two components of a bisector; uniqueness of the $L^2$
$D$-potential; and the factor $\ell(I)$ obtained from
$(\ell(I)D)^{-1}m$.  The tail calculation is an explicit expansion of the
Hilbert-transform kernel and uses no external theorem.

On August 11, 2026, the local run indexes were searched by arXiv id, exact
title, Remark~7.3 terminology, and the adapted-molecule phrase.  Exact-phrase
web searches found copies of the source memoir but no later answer, and
sign/Hilbert-transform searches found no matching resolution.  This was a
bounded search, so novelty confidence is moderate pending a specialist
citation review.
