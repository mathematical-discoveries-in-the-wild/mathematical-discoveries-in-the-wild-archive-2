\section{Source question}

The source is F. P. Baudier, B. M. Braga, I. Farah, A. Vignati, and
R. Willett, \emph{Coarse equivalence versus bijective coarse equivalence of
expander graphs}, arXiv:2307.11529, published in \emph{Mathematische
Zeitschrift} 307 (2024), Paper 44 \cite{source}.

Their Theorem 1.2 concerns coarse disjoint unions
$X=\bigsqcup_n X_n$ and $Y=\bigsqcup_n Y_n$ of expander graphs and a coarse
equivalence $f:X\to Y$.  It says that the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f$ is close to a bijective coarse equivalence;
\item after discarding finitely many components, $f$ matches components of
equal cardinality, with the total cardinalities of the discarded parts also
equal.
\end{enumerate}
Immediately afterward the authors write:
\begin{quote}
``We do not know whether the conclusion of Theorem 1.2 can fail if $X$ and
$Y$ are coarse disjoint unions of finite connected graphs (that are not
necessarily expanders).''
\end{quote}
This occurs across printed pages 3--4.  The complete source evidence follows.

\section{Definitions}

Two maps $u,v:S\to T$ into a metric space are \emph{close} if
$\sup_{x\in S}d(u(x),v(x))<\infty$.  A map $u:S\to T$ is \emph{coarse} if
for every $r>0$ there is $s>0$ such that
\[
 d_S(x,x')<r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_T(u(x),u(x'))<s.
\]
It is a \emph{coarse equivalence} if it has a coarse inverse up to closeness.
A \emph{bijective coarse equivalence} is a coarse equivalence that is also a
bijection.

A coarse disjoint union of finite metric spaces $(P_n)$ is their disjoint
union with a metric restricting to the given metric on each component and
satisfying $d(P_n,P_m)\to\infty$ as $(n,m)\to\infty$ with $n\ne m$.

\section{Idea of the proof}

Equal component cardinalities control only total mass; expansion is what lets
the source theorem redistribute that mass by a uniformly bounded matching.
Paths have large pieces with tiny boundary, so one can create a persistent
local mass deficit while compensating elsewhere in the same component.

On a path of $4n$ vertices, compress the first half by a factor of two.  Map
the second half with slope $3/2$, so that the total image remains uniformly
dense.  The resulting maps and explicit inverse sections are uniformly
Lipschitz and inverse to within one, hence define a coarse self-equivalence of
the disjoint union.  Component sizes match exactly.  But any map within a
fixed distance $C$ must send the first $2n$ vertices into only
$n+O(C)$ possible targets.  No injection, and therefore no bijection, can do
this for arbitrarily large $n$.

\section{Construction}

For $n\geq1$, let
\[
 P_n=\{0,1,\ldots,4n-1\}
\]
with the path metric.  Put $X=\bigsqcup_{n\geq1}P_n$ and define
\[
 d((n,k),(m,j))=
 \begin{cases}
 |k-j|,&n=m,\\
 4n+4m,&n\ne m.
 \end{cases}                                                   \tag{1}
\]

For $k\in P_n$, define
\[
 f_n(k)=
 \begin{cases}
 \lfloor k/2\rfloor,&0\leq k<2n,\\
 n+\lfloor3(k-2n)/2\rfloor,&2n\leq k<4n,
 \end{cases}                                                   \tag{2}
\]
and, for $j\in P_n$, define
\[
 g_n(j)=
 \begin{cases}
 2j,&0\leq j<n,\\
 2n+\lfloor2(j-n)/3\rfloor,&n\leq j<4n.
 \end{cases}                                                   \tag{3}
\]
Let $f,g:X\to X$ act by $f_n,g_n$ on $P_n$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:space}
Equation \emph{(1)} defines a uniformly locally finite metric, and $X$ is a
coarse disjoint union of the finite connected graphs $P_n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The only nontrivial triangle check is when two vertices lie in one component
and the third lies in another.  Since $\operatorname{diam}(P_n)=4n-1$ while
the two cross-component legs have sum at least $8n+8$, the triangle
inequality holds.  The restriction to $P_n$ is its path metric, and
$d(P_n,P_m)=4n+4m\to\infty$ off the diagonal, so this is a coarse disjoint
union.

Fix $R>0$.  A ball of radius $R$ meets at most $2\lfloor R\rfloor+1$
vertices in its own path.  If it meets another $P_m$, then
$4n+4m\leq R$, hence $m\leq R/4$.  Thus its cardinality is bounded by
\[
 2\lfloor R\rfloor+1+\sum_{m\leq R/4}4m,
\]
independently of its center.  Hence $X$ is uniformly locally finite.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:coarse}
The maps $f$ and $g$ are $2$-Lipschitz and
\[
 d(g(f(x)),x)\leq1,
 \qquad
 d(f(g(x)),x)\leq1
 \quad (x\in X).
\]
Consequently $f$ is a coarse equivalence with coarse inverse $g$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Along a component, consecutive values of $f_n$ differ by at most $2$:
the first part of (2) has increments $0$ or $1$, the transition has increment
$1$, and the second part has increments $1$ or $2$.  Consecutive values of
$g_n$ differ by at most $2$ as well.  Between distinct components, both maps
preserve the component indices, so they preserve the distance $4n+4m$
exactly.  Thus both maps are globally $2$-Lipschitz.

For $k<2n$,
\[
 g_n(f_n(k))=2\lfloor k/2\rfloor,
\]
which differs from $k$ by at most $1$.  If $k=2n+r$, then
\[
 g_n(f_n(k))
 =2n+\left\lfloor\frac{2\lfloor3r/2\rfloor}{3}\right\rfloor,
\]
whose final term equals $r$ for even $r$ and $r-1$ for odd $r$.

Similarly, $f_n(g_n(j))=j$ for $j<n$.  If $j=n+s$, then
\[
 f_n(g_n(j))
 =n+\left\lfloor\frac{3\lfloor2s/3\rfloor}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
Writing $s$ modulo $3$ shows that the final term is $s$ when
$s\equiv0\pmod3$ and $s-1$ otherwise.  Both composition errors are therefore
at most $1$.
\end{proof}

\section{The bounded-displacement obstruction}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
The coarse equivalence $f:X\to X$ satisfies condition \emph{(2)} of the
source Theorem 1.2 with all components retained and the identity matching of
components.  Nevertheless, $f$ is not close to any bijection $X\to X$.
In particular, it is not close to any bijective coarse equivalence.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The component condition is immediate: $f(P_n)\subseteq P_n$ and
$|P_n|=|P_n|$ for every $n$.  In the notation of the source theorem, take
$N=M=\N$ and $i(n)=n$; both discarded sums are zero.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that a bijection $h:X\to X$ and a constant
$C<\infty$ satisfy
\[
 d(h(x),f(x))\leq C\qquad(x\in X).                         \tag{4}
\]
Choose an integer $n>\max\{C,1\}$.  Since $f(P_n)\subseteq P_n$ and every
point outside $P_n$ is at distance at least $4n+4>C$ from $P_n$, (4) implies
$h(P_n)\subseteq P_n$.

Let
\[
 A_n=\{0,\ldots,2n-1\}\subseteq P_n,
 \qquad B_n=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\subseteq P_n.
\]
By (2), $f(A_n)=B_n$.  Equation (4) therefore forces
\[
 h(A_n)\subseteq N_C(B_n)\cap P_n
 \subseteq\{0,\ldots,n-1+\lfloor C\rfloor\}.
\]
The set on the right has at most $n+\lfloor C\rfloor<2n=|A_n|$ elements.
This contradicts injectivity of $h$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Negative answer to the source question]
The conclusion of the source Theorem 1.2 fails for coarse disjoint unions of
finite connected graphs.  It already fails when every component is a path and
$X=Y$.
\end{corollary}

\section{Limitations and bounded novelty audit}

This packet answers only the combinatorial/geometric question after Theorem
1.2.  It does not claim that the counterexample is induced by an isomorphism
of uniform Roe algebras.  The latter rigidity conjecture, stated separately
as Conjecture 1.3 in the source, has independent later resolutions and is not
the target here.

On 9 August 2026, the four run indexes were searched for arXiv:2307.11529,
the exact question phrase, and the core terms ``coarse disjoint union'',
``finite connected graphs'', and ``bijective coarse equivalence''.  Exact and
keyword web/arXiv searches were also run.  The latest source page still
contains the question.  Finally, the OpenAlex record, updated 23 July 2026,
listed two citing works; inspection of their titles, abstracts, and searchable
text found only operator-algebraic applications to expanders, not the present
finite-path construction or an answer to this question.  No prior answer was
found within these bounds.  This supports plausible novelty but is not a
certification.

\section{Human-review recommendation}

\textbf{Recommendation: promote after expert confirmation.}  The main review
points are: (i) that the source's phrase ``the conclusion of Theorem 1.2 can
fail'' is correctly read as asking whether the stated equivalence can fail;
(ii) the global metric in (1) meets the source definition of coarse disjoint
union; and (iii) the counting argument excludes every bounded-distance
bijection, not merely componentwise permutations.
