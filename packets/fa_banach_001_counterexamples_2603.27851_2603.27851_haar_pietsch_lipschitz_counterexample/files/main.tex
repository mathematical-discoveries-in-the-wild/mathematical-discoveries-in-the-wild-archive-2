\section*{Result Status}

\textbf{Candidate full counterexample, likely valid, pending expert review.}
For every \(1\le p<\infty\), the construction below answers the open problem
repeated in Bispo--Macedo--Santos \cite{BMS} in the negative.  It also answers
the original 2012 formulation of Botelho--Pellegrino--Rueda--Santos--Seoane-
Sep\'ulveda \cite{BPRSS}.

\section{Source Problem}

Let \(G\) be a compact Hausdorff topological group, let \(H\) be a closed
translation-invariant subspace of \(C(G)\), let \(Y\) be a metric space, and
let \(u:H\to Y\) be a translation-invariant Lipschitz \(p\)-summing map.  The
question asks whether normalized Haar measure \(\sigma_G\) must be a Pietsch
measure for \(u\) \cite[p.~2, Introduction, Open Problem]{BMS}.

In the pairwise form used in \cite[Section 4]{BMS}, this would mean that there
is a finite constant \(C\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:haar-domination}
 d_Y(u(f),u(g))
 \le C\left(\int_G |f(x)-g(x)|^p\,d\sigma_G(x)\right)^{1/p}
 \qquad(f,g\in H).
\end{equation}
The counterexample below rules out even domination with an arbitrary finite
constant, and therefore also rules out the sharper convention in which the
constant must be \(\pi_p^L(u)\).

\section{Proof Intuition}

The scalar-valued case exposes a simple gap between the hypothesis and the
desired conclusion.  Every base-point-preserving real-valued Lipschitz
function on a metric space is automatically Lipschitz \(p\)-summing: after
normalization, the function itself is one of the test functions over which
the defining supremum is taken.

On \(C(G)\), the supremum norm is such a scalar-valued Lipschitz function, and
it is invariant under translations of \(G\).  Haar domination for this map
would force the supremum norm on \(C(G)\) to be bounded by its \(L^p\)-norm.
Continuous peaks on the circle have supremum one and arbitrarily small
\(L^p\)-norm, giving the contradiction.

\section{Counterexample}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
Fix \(1\le p<\infty\).  Let \(G=\T\), let
\(H=C(\T,\R)\), let \(Y=\R\) with its usual metric, and define
\[
 u:H\longrightarrow\R,\qquad u(f)=\norm{f}_{\infty}.
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(H\) is a closed translation-invariant subspace of \(C(\T)\);
\item \(u\) is translation invariant;
\item \(u\) is Lipschitz \(p\)-summing, in fact \(\pi_p^L(u)=1\);
\item normalized Haar measure on \(\T\) is not a Pietsch measure for \(u\).
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, the answer to the source open problem is negative for every
finite \(p\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first assertion is immediate.  For \(\psi\in\T\), the translation
\(I_\psi(z)=\psi z\) is a bijection of \(\T\), and hence
\[
 u(f\circ I_\psi)=\norm{f\circ I_\psi}_\infty
 =\norm{f}_\infty=u(f).
\]
Thus \(u\) is translation invariant.

We next verify the summing condition directly.  The reverse triangle
inequality gives
\[
 |u(f)-u(g)|\le \norm{f-g}_\infty,
\]
so \(u(0)=0\) and \(u\) belongs to the unit ball of the Lipschitz dual
\(H^\#=\Lip_0(H,\R)\).  Therefore, for arbitrary
\(f_1,\ldots,f_m,g_1,\ldots,g_m\in H\),
\begin{align*}
 \left(\sum_{j=1}^m |u(f_j)-u(g_j)|^p\right)^{1/p}
 &\le
 \sup_{v\in B_{H^\#}}
 \left(\sum_{j=1}^m |v(f_j)-v(g_j)|^p\right)^{1/p}.
\end{align*}
This is exactly the Farmer--Johnson Lipschitz \(p\)-summing inequality, with
constant one.  Taking \(m=1\), \(f_1\equiv1\), and \(g_1=0\) shows that the
best constant is one, so \(\pi_p^L(u)=1\).

It remains to disprove Haar domination.  Write a point of \(\T\) as
\(e^{it}\), with \(-\pi\le t\le\pi\), and let normalized Haar measure be
\(dt/(2\pi)\).  For \(n\ge1\), define the continuous peak
\[
 f_n(e^{it})=(1-n|t|)_+.
\]
The endpoint values agree, so this is a well-defined continuous function on
the circle.  Moreover,
\[
 \norm{f_n}_\infty=1
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \int_\T |f_n|^p\,d\sigma_\T
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-1/n}^{1/n}(1-n|t|)^p\,dt
 =\frac1{\pi n(p+1)}.
\]
If normalized Haar measure were a Pietsch measure for \(u\), then applying
\eqref{eq:haar-domination} to \((f_n,0)\) would give, for some finite \(C\),
\[
 1=|u(f_n)-u(0)|
 \le C\left(\frac1{\pi n(p+1)}\right)^{1/p}
 \qquad(n\ge1),
\]
which is impossible as \(n\to\infty\).  Thus Haar measure is not a Pietsch
measure for \(u\).
\end{proof}

\section{Robustness and Boundary Checks}

\begin{remark}[Why the source's partial theorem is not contradicted]
Bispo--Macedo--Santos prove Haar domination for the smaller class of
Lipschitz \(p\)-\(B_{H^*}\)-summing maps \cite[Theorem 4.3]{BMS}.  The map
above cannot belong to that smaller class: their theorem would then imply
\eqref{eq:haar-domination}, which the peaks disprove.  Thus the example lands
exactly in the gap between ordinary Lipschitz \(p\)-summability, tested by
the nonlinear Lipschitz dual \(H^\#\), and their linear-dual hypothesis.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[No dependence on the optimal constant]
The contradiction uses only the nonexistence of any finite domination
constant.  It is therefore unaffected by differing conventions for the term
``Pietsch measure.''
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[More general form]
The same construction works on any infinite compact group that admits
continuous functions of supremum one with Haar \(L^p\)-norm tending to zero.
The circle was chosen to make this concentration explicit and avoid any
auxiliary topological-measure lemma.
\end{remark}

\section{Novelty Check}

The March 2026 source paper still states the question as open and conjectures
that its \(B_{H^*}\)-based partial theorem is the best possible answer
\cite[Remark following Theorem 4.3]{BMS}.  Exact-title, arXiv-id, and
translation-invariant Lipschitz-summing searches through August 9, 2026 found
no later primary source recording this counterexample or another resolution.
This is evidence of novelty, not a claim of exhaustive bibliographic proof.
