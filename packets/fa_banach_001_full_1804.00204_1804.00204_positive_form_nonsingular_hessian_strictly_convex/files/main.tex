\textbf{Source question.} Friedland--Gaubert ask in Remark 1 after their
Theorem 9 (PDF page 12) whether positivity away from the origin together with
invertibility of the Hessian away from the origin forces strict convexity for
an even-degree homogeneous form \cite{FG}.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/question_crop.png}
\end{center}

\textbf{Answer.} Yes. In fact the Hessian must be positive definite at every
nonzero point, and none of the surrounding tensor nonnegativity or
irreducibility assumptions is needed.

\textbf{Proof intuition.} Minimize the form on the unit sphere. At a minimizer,
the constrained second-derivative test makes the Hessian positive in every
tangent direction. Homogeneity supplies a positive radial eigenvalue and
kills the radial--tangential cross terms, so the Hessian is positive definite
at this one point. A continuous nonsingular symmetric matrix cannot change
its inertia along a path. Since punctured Euclidean space is connected in
dimension at least two, positivity propagates everywhere.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $F:\R^n\to\R$ be a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d>1$. Assume
\[
 F(x)>0\quad(x\ne0),
 \qquad
 \det \nabla^2F(x)\ne0\quad(x\ne0).
\]
Then $d$ is even, $\nabla^2F(x)$ is positive definite for every $x\ne0$,
and $F$ is strictly convex on $\R^n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Positivity at both $x$ and $-x$ forces the polynomial degree $d$ to be even.
First suppose $n\ge2$. Compactness of the unit sphere gives a point
$u\in S^{n-1}$ at which $F|_{S^{n-1}}$ is minimal. Put
$m=F(u)>0$. The Lagrange-multiplier equation and Euler's identity give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrange}
 \nabla F(u)=\lambda u,
 \qquad
 \lambda=u^{T}\nabla F(u)=dF(u)=dm>0.
\end{equation}
The constrained second-derivative test says that, for every $v\perp u$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tangent}
 v^T\bigl(\nabla^2F(u)-\lambda I\bigr)v\ge0,
 \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
 v^T\nabla^2F(u)v\ge\lambda\lVert v\rVert^2.
\end{equation}

Differentiating Euler's identity $x^T\nabla F(x)=dF(x)$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler2}
 \nabla^2F(x)x=(d-1)\nabla F(x).
\end{equation}
At $u$, equations \eqref{eq:lagrange}--\eqref{eq:euler2} show that $u$ is a
Hessian eigenvector with eigenvalue $(d-1)\lambda>0$. They also show that
$u^T\nabla^2F(u)v=0$ whenever $v\perp u$. Thus, for $a\in\R$ and
$v\perp u$,
\[
 (au+v)^T\nabla^2F(u)(au+v)
 \ge (d-1)\lambda a^2+\lambda\lVert v\rVert^2,
\]
which is positive unless $a=0$ and $v=0$. Therefore $\nabla^2F(u)$ is
positive definite.

The inertia of a continuous family of real symmetric nonsingular matrices is
locally constant: an eigenvalue would have to pass through zero in order to
change sign. The set $\R^n\setminus\{0\}$ is connected for $n\ge2$, so the
inertia of $\nabla^2F(x)$ is constant there. Since it is $(n,0,0)$ at $u$,
$\nabla^2F(x)$ is positive definite at every nonzero $x$.

If $n=1$, homogeneity gives $F(x)=a x^d$ with $a>0$ and even $d$, so
$F''(x)=ad(d-1)x^{d-2}>0$ for $x\ne0$; the same conclusion holds.

Finally take distinct $x,y\in\R^n$ and set
$g(t)=F((1-t)x+ty)$ for $0\le t\le1$. Then
\[
 g''(t)=(y-x)^T\nabla^2F((1-t)x+ty)(y-x)>0
\]
except possibly at the single value of $t$ for which $(1-t)x+ty=0$.
Consequently $g'$ is strictly increasing, so $g$ is strictly convex. This on
every line segment is exactly strict convexity of $F$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[strength and scope]
The proof only needs a $C^2$ positive homogeneous function on
$\R^n\setminus\{0\}$ with a continuous Hessian and the usual behavior at the
origin needed for the last line-segment argument. For the polynomial question
as printed, all hypotheses are automatic. In particular, tensor
nonnegativity, weak irreducibility, and the restriction $d>2$ play no role.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Human-review recommendation.} Check equations
