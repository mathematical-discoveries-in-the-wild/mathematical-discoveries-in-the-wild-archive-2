\section*{Source question}

B. Rubin, \emph{Funk, Cosine, and Sine Transforms on Stiefel and Grassmann
manifolds}, arXiv:1009.2184, Conjecture 6.8 on PDF page 38.
For a smooth right $O(m)$-invariant function $f$ on $\V$ (with $2m\le n$ in
the composition/Funk setting), the conjecture asks whether the normalized
cosine family $\mathcal M^\alpha f$
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] extends pointwise meromorphically with possible polar set
  $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$;
\item[(b)] is smooth and right $O(m)$-invariant away from $\N$;
\item[(c)] satisfies, pointwise,
  \[
  \mathcal M^{2m-\alpha-n}\mathcal M^\alpha f=f
  \quad
  (\alpha,2m-\alpha-n\notin\N);
  \]
\item[(d)] gives the pointwise Funk inversion
  $\mathcal M^{2m-n}F_m f=\varkappa_m f$.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Exact later answer}

B. Rubin, \emph{Funk, Cosine, and Sine Transforms on Stiefel and Grassmann
manifolds, II}, arXiv:1103.1161.  Its abstract and introduction on PDF pages
1--2 say that the Part-I identities, previously proved only as distributions,
are now obtained pointwise, and that the paper answers the corresponding
questions from Part I.

The clauses match as follows.  Theorem 3.1(iv), on PDF pages 7--8, proves that
the cosine transform divided by $\Gamma_m(\alpha/2)$ is entire in $\alpha$
and smooth in the Stiefel variable.  After multiplication by the remaining
normalizing factor, the paragraph before Theorem 6.4 on PDF page 13 states
that $\mathcal M^\alpha f$ is defined for every $\alpha\notin\N$ and is a
smooth function.  This proves (a)--(b) in the intended possible-pole sense.
Theorem 6.4 on PDF pages 13--14 is exactly the pointwise identity in (c).
Finally, Theorem 6.1 on PDF pages 12--13 gives the pointwise inversion formula
for $F_{m,k}$; taking $k=m$ is exactly clause (d), with the same constant.

\section*{Identification and scope}

The sequel cites Part I as its reference [34], uses the same operators and
normalization, and explicitly presents its results as pointwise upgrades of
Part I.  This is therefore an author-identified answer, not a new deduction.
The words ``the polar set'' cannot literally assert that every positive
integer is an actual pole for every $f$ (already $f=0$ is a counterexample);
both papers use $\N$ as the universal possible/excluded polar set.  Part II
also poses new problems, including explicit Wallach-set measures and
higher-rank Sobolev range descriptions; those are separate and are not
claimed resolved here.
