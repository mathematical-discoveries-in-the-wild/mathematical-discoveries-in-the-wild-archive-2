\noindent\textbf{Status.}
\texttt{literature\_already\_answered (two deterministic questions)}.

\section*{Source questions}
Volcic \cite{Volcic} asks on PDF pages 12--13 whether there exist a uniformly
distributed sequence of directions $(u_m)$ and a bounded Caccioppoli, convex,
compact, summable, or finite-measure set $F$ such that its iterated Steiner
symmetrals fail to converge to the centered equimeasurable ball in the
appropriate metric.  He expects a negative answer and makes the stronger
conjecture that density alone of $(u_m)$ suffices for convergence.

\section*{Later counterexample}
Bianchi, Burchard, Gronchi, and Volcic \cite{BBGV} give exactly the requested
example.  In their Example~2.1 (PDF pages 3--4), choose
$(\alpha_m)\subset(0,\pi/2)$ with
\[
  \sum_m\alpha_m=\infty,
  \qquad \sum_m\alpha_m^2<\infty,
  \qquad
  \beta_m=\sum_{k=1}^m\alpha_k,
  \qquad
  u_m=(\cos\beta_m,\sin\beta_m).
\]
Writing $\gamma=\prod_m\cos\alpha_m>0$, they take a planar convex body $K$
of area smaller than a disk of diameter $\gamma$ which contains a vertical
unit segment.  Successive symmetrizations preserve a rotating segment whose
length tends to $\gamma$.  If the symmetrals converged in Hausdorff distance,
their limit would contain a disk of diameter $\gamma$, contradicting
preservation of the smaller area of $K$.  Hence the symmetrals do not converge.

Section~5 of the same paper (PDF page 9) makes this example uniformly
distributed.  Take, for instance,
\[
  \alpha_m=\vartheta m^{-\sigma},
  \qquad \frac12<\sigma<1,
  \qquad 0<\vartheta<\frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
Then $\alpha_m\downarrow0$, $m\alpha_m\to\infty$, and
$\sum_m\alpha_m^2<\infty$.  The cited uniform-distribution criterion gives
that $(u_m)$ is uniformly distributed on $S^1$, yet the symmetrals of the
compact convex $K$ from Example~2.1 do not converge.

\section*{Resolution and scope}
Therefore the source existence question has answer \textbf{yes}, already for
a bounded compact convex planar body in the Hausdorff metric.  Since every
uniformly distributed sequence is dense, the same example disproves the
stronger density-only convergence conjecture.  This is an explicit
later-literature resolution, not a new result.  It does not settle the source
paper's separate questions about unbounded sets, nonuniform symmetric random
laws, or weakened independence.
