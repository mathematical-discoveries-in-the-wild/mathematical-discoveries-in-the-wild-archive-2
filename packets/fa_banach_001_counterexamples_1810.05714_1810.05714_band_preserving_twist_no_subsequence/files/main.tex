\section{The source question}

Throughout, functions are real equivalence classes modulo null sets.  A normed
space $X(\mu)\subset L^0(\mu)$ is \emph{rectangular} if there is $C$ such that
\[
 f\in X,\ A\in\Sigma
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \one_Af\in X,\qquad \|\one_Af\|_X\le C\|f\|_X.
\]
It has the \emph{subsequence property} if every norm-convergent sequence has a
subsequence converging pointwise almost everywhere to the same limit.

Calabuig, Fern\'andez Unzueta, Galaz-Fontes, and S\'anchez P\'erez ask at the
start of Section 4.2:
\begin{quote}
\emph{We do not know if an arbitrary Banach rectangular f.s.\ has the
subsequence property.}
\end{quote}
They prove the assertion for rectangular sequence spaces in Proposition 4.5
\cite[Section 4.2]{CFGS}.  The source passage, on PDF page 12, is reproduced in
Figure~\ref{fig:question}.

\section{Proof intuition}

Rectangularity only sees multiplication by characteristic functions.  On the
nonatomic space $[0,1]$, that Boolean action does not determine the usual
measurable realization of a Banach space continuously.  Local Hamel bases of
the universally complete lattice $L^0$ allow a discontinuous real-linear
change of coordinates which nevertheless commutes with every characteristic
projection.

Choose such a coordinate involution $U$ which swaps the constant function
$\one$ with $q(t)=t$.  The image $U(L^2)$, equipped with the transported
$L^2$ norm, is automatically complete.  Since $U$ commutes with restrictions,
the norm is even strictly rectangular.  But on simple functions $s$ one has
$U(s)=qs$, whereas $U(q)=\one$.  Approximating $q$ by simple functions proves
that $U:L^2\to L^0$ is discontinuous.  A norm-null sequence can therefore stay
uniformly away from zero in measure, which prevents almost-everywhere
convergence along every subsequence.

\section{The local coordinate involution}

Let
\[
 (\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)=([0,1],\mathcal L,\cL^1),
 \qquad G=L^0(\mu),\qquad q(t)=t.
\]
The vector lattice $G$ is universally complete.  Its band projections are
exactly
\[
 P_Af=\one_Af\qquad(A\in\Sigma),
\]
where sets are understood modulo null sets.

We use the local Hamel basis theorem for universally complete vector lattices
from Gutman, Kusraev, and Kutateladze
\cite[Sections 1.1--1.2]{GKK}.  A locally linearly independent set extends to
a local Hamel basis $\cE$.  Every $f\in G$ then has, on a partition of unity,
a finite linear expansion in the restrictions of elements of $\cE$, and the
local coefficients are unique
\cite[Sections 1.2.1--1.2.3]{GKK}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:involution}
There is a real-linear involution $U:G\to G$ such that
\[
 U(\one)=q,\qquad U(q)=\one,\qquad
 U P_A=P_AU\quad(A\in\Sigma).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, $\{\one,q\}$ is locally linearly independent.  Indeed, if
$A$ has positive measure and
\[
 \one_A(a\one+bq)=0,
\]
then $a+bt=0$ for almost every $t\in A$.  If $b\ne0$, this would put $A$
modulo a null set inside the singleton $\{-a/b\}$, which is impossible.
Thus $b=0$ and then $a=0$.

Extend $\{\one,q\}$ to a local Hamel basis $\cE$ of $G$.  Let
$\sigma:\cE\to\cE$ swap $\one$ and $q$ and fix all other basis elements.
For $f\in G$, choose a local representation
\[
 f=\sum_{\xi}\one_{A_\xi}
       \left(\sum_{e\in F_\xi}\lambda_{\xi,e}e\right),
 \tag{1}
\]
where $(A_\xi)$ is a measurable partition modulo null sets and every
$F_\xi\subset\cE$ is finite.  Define
\[
 Uf=\sum_{\xi}\one_{A_\xi}
       \left(\sum_{e\in F_\xi}\lambda_{\xi,e}\sigma(e)\right).
 \tag{2}
\]
The uniqueness of local coefficients shows, after passing to a common
refinement, that (2) is independent of (1).  The same common-refinement
argument gives linearity.  Since $\sigma^2$ is the identity, so is $U^2$.
Finally, restricting (1) to a measurable set before or after applying (2)
gives the same local expansion.  Hence $UP_A=P_AU$ for every $A$.  The two
displayed values of $U$ follow from the definition of $\sigma$.
\end{proof}

\section{The counterexample}

Define the linear subspace
\[
 X:=U\bigl(L^2[0,1]\bigr)\subset L^0[0,1]
\]
and, using the bijectivity of $U$, define
\[
 \|x\|_X:=\|U^{-1}x\|_2\qquad(x\in X).
\]

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
$X$ is a Banach space of measurable functions with a strictly rectangular
norm, but $X$ does not have the subsequence property.  Consequently the
question in Section 4.2 of \cite{CFGS} has a negative answer.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The map
\[
 U:L^2[0,1]\longrightarrow X
\]
is an isometric linear bijection by construction, so $X$ is complete.  For
$f\in L^2$ and $A\in\Sigma$, Lemma~\ref{lem:involution} gives
\[
 P_AU(f)=U(P_Af)\in X
\]
and
\[
 \|P_AU(f)\|_X
 =\|P_Af\|_2
 \le\|f\|_2
 =\|U(f)\|_X.
 \tag{3}
\]
Thus $X$ is strictly rectangular.

We next show that the linear map
\[
 T:=U|_{L^2}:L^2[0,1]\longrightarrow L^0[0,1],
 \tag{4}
\]
where $L^0$ has the topology of convergence in measure, is discontinuous.
For every simple function
$s=\sum_{j=1}^m c_j\one_{A_j}$, the commutation relation in
Lemma~\ref{lem:involution} yields
\[
 U(s)
 =\sum_{j=1}^m c_jP_{A_j}U(\one)
 =\sum_{j=1}^m c_j\one_{A_j}q
 =sq.
 \tag{5}
\]
Choose simple functions $s_n\to q$ in $L^2$.  By (5),
$U(s_n)=s_nq\to q^2$ in $L^2$, hence in measure.  If (4) were continuous,
the same sequence would converge in measure to
$U(q)=\one$.  Uniqueness of limits in $L^0$ would give $q^2=\one$ almost
everywhere, a contradiction.

Since (4) is not continuous at zero and the sets
\[
 V_{\varepsilon,\delta}
 :=\{g\in L^0:\mu(|g|>\varepsilon)<\delta\}
\]
form a neighborhood basis at zero, there are fixed
$\varepsilon,\delta>0$ and functions $f_n\in L^2$ such that
\[
 \|f_n\|_2<\frac1n,\qquad
 \mu\bigl(|U(f_n)|>\varepsilon\bigr)\ge\delta
 \quad(n\ge1).
 \tag{6}
\]
Put $x_n=U(f_n)\in X$.  Then $\|x_n\|_X=\|f_n\|_2\to0$.  No subsequence of
$(x_n)$ converges almost everywhere to zero: on a finite measure space such
convergence would imply convergence in measure, contradicting the uniform
lower bound in (6).  Hence $X$ fails the subsequence property.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
The positive cone $X_+=\{x\in X:x\ge0\text{ a.e.}\}$ is not closed.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For Banach rectangular function spaces, source Lemma 2.8 says that closedness
of the positive cone is equivalent to the subsequence property
\cite[Lemma 2.8]{CFGS}.  Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample}.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification, provenance, and limitations}

The only nonconstructive ingredient is the extension of $\{\one,q\}$ to a
local Hamel basis.  After that extension, the involution is given explicitly
on local coordinates, and all norm estimates are identities.  The example is
real-linear, uses the finite Lebesgue measure space required by the source,
and satisfies rectangularity with the best constant $C=1$.

The older Wickstead paper supplies the local basis representation, but does
not state this transported-$L^2$ construction or connect it to the later
rectangular-space question.  A bounded novelty search covered the run indexes,
the exact sentence and title from the source, and combinations of the terms
\emph{rectangular function space}, \emph{subsequence property}, and
\emph{band preserving}.  It found no later claimed resolution.  This supports
plausible novelty but is not a definitive certification.

The construction is choice-dependent and gives no concrete formula for $U$
on arbitrary measurable functions.  It does not affect the positive sequence
space result in \cite{CFGS}; the nonatomic measure algebra is essential to the
local coordinate pathology.

\paragraph{Human-review recommendation.}
Review as a likely valid full counterexample.  The decisive audit is
Lemma~\ref{lem:involution}: verify that permutation of a local Hamel basis
defines an everywhere-defined band-preserving linear involution.  Once this is
accepted, (3)--(6) are elementary.
