\title{Curved projective levels force maximal norm growth}
\author{Automated proof attempt for arXiv:2308.10582}
\date{August 13, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We strengthen the stationary-phase criterion in the source paper: a
nondegenerate extremum is unnecessary.  Maximal $L^p$ multiplier-norm growth
already follows if one regular level hypersurface of the homogeneous phase
has nonzero projective Gauss--Kronecker curvature at one point.  This gives a
complete negative answer to the source question in dimension three.  Using a
compact-developable classification theorem of Ishikawa--Morimoto, it also
gives a negative answer for even phases in dimensions four and six.  Thus any
affirmative example must have every regular projective level developable; the
existence of such a global phase, and the required analytic upper bound, remain
open in the unrestricted higher-dimensional case.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

\section{Source question and exact scope}

Let $\Phi\in C^\infty(\R^d\setminus\{0\},\R)$ be homogeneous of degree zero,
and let $T_\Phi^t$ be the Fourier multiplier with symbol $e^{it\Phi}$.  Closing
Remark 1 of Bulj~\cite{Bulj} asks exactly:
\begin{quote}
``When $d\ge 3$, does there exist a nonconstant unimodular homogeneous
function $\Phi\in C^\infty(\R^d\setminus\{0\},\R)$ of degree $0$ such that
\[
 \liminf_{t\to\infty}
 \frac{\|T_\Phi^t\|_{L^p\to L^p}}
 {t^{d|\frac12-\frac1p|}}=0\,?
\]''
\end{quote}
The exact source context is reproduced below.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.97\textwidth]{figures/open_problem_crop.png}
\end{center}

The question is understood for a fixed $p\in(1,\infty)$.  At $p=2$ the
operator is unitary and the denominator has exponent zero, so the answer is
trivially no.  The results below apply simultaneously to every such $p$.

\section{Proof intuition}

The source changes variables from frequency $\xi$ to an affine direction
$u\in\R^{d-1}$ and a radial coordinate $r$.  At a stationary point, the
Hessian of the transformed phase is not $H\phi$ but the bordered matrix
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}H\phi&-\nabla\phi\\
                 -\nabla\phi^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
At a regular point this matrix is invertible exactly when the Hessian of
$\phi$ restricted to the tangent space of its level set is nondegenerate.
That is precisely nonzero Gauss--Kronecker curvature of the projective level.
The source's implicit-function and stationary-phase proof then works locally
without any critical point of $\phi$.

In dimension three, a regular level curve failing this criterion everywhere
must be a great circle.  Two different regular values would therefore give
two disjoint great circles, which is impossible.  For even phases in
dimensions four and six, the same intersection argument uses the theorem that
compact developable hypersurfaces in $\RP^3$ and $\RP^5$ are projective
hyperplanes.

\section{The bordered-Hessian criterion}

Write a central affine chart as
\[
 \phi(u)=\Phi(u,1),\qquad u\in\R^{d-1}.
\]
At a regular point $u_0$, put $a=\nabla\phi(u_0)\ne0$ and
$E=a^\perp\subset\R^{d-1}$.

\begin{lemma}[Bordered determinant]\label{lem:border}
The matrix
\[
 B(u_0)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 H\phi(u_0)&-a\\-a^{\mathsf T}&0
 \end{pmatrix}
\]
is invertible if and only if the quadratic form
$H\phi(u_0)|_E$ is nondegenerate.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose an orthonormal basis whose first $d-2$ vectors span $E$ and whose last
vector is $a/|a|$.  In that basis, for some matrix $C$, vector $b$, and scalar
$h$, the bordered matrix is
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 C&b&0\\ b^{\mathsf T}&h&-|a|\\0&-|a|&0
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Expanding in the last row and then the last column shows that its determinant
is nonzero exactly when $\det C\ne0$.  The matrix $C$ represents
$H\phi(u_0)|_E$.
\end{proof}

For the regular hypersurface $\{\phi=\phi(u_0)\}$, the second fundamental
form in this affine chart is, up to the nonzero factor $|a|^{-1}$,
$H\phi(u_0)|_E$.  Thus Lemma~\ref{lem:border} is equivalently the existence of
nonzero Gauss--Kronecker curvature.  Nondegeneracy of the projective second
fundamental form is invariant under changing central charts.

\begin{theorem}[Curved regular level]\label{thm:curved}
Let $d\ge2$, $p\in(1,\infty)$, and let $\Phi$ be a smooth real nonconstant
degree-zero homogeneous phase.  Suppose that at some regular point of
$\Phi|_{S^{d-1}}$, a regular projective level hypersurface has nondegenerate
second fundamental form.  Then there is $c=c(d,p,\Phi)>0$ such that
\[
 \|T_\Phi^t\|_{L^p\to L^p}
 \ge c\,t^{d|\frac12-\frac1p|}
 \qquad(t\ge1).
\]
In particular, the normalized liminf in the source question is positive.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Rotate coordinates and choose a central chart containing the given point.
With $u_0$ and $a$ as above, consider the transformed phase used in the source
paper,
\[
 \Psi_x(u,r)=\phi(u)+r(u\cdot x_-+x_d).
\]
Set
\[
 r_0=1,\qquad x_-^0=-a,\qquad x_d^0=u_0\cdot a.
\]
Then $(u_0,1)$ is a stationary point of $\Psi_{x^0}$, since
\[
 \nabla_u\Psi_{x^0}(u_0,1)=a+x_-^0=0,
 \qquad
 \partial_r\Psi_{x^0}(u_0,1)=u_0\cdot x_-^0+x_d^0=0.
\]
Its full $(u,r)$ Hessian is
\[
 H_{(u,r)}\Psi_{x^0}(u_0,1)=
 \begin{pmatrix}H\phi(u_0)&-a\\-a^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
which is invertible by Lemma~\ref{lem:border}.

Apply the implicit-function theorem to
$F((u,r),x)=\nabla_{(u,r)}\Psi_x(u,r)$.  After shrinking neighbourhoods, there
are open sets $U\ni x^0$ and $V\ni(u_0,1)$ and a unique smooth map
$g:U\to V$ such that $g(x)$ is the unique stationary point in $V$, and its
Hessian stays invertible.  Choose the Fourier transform of a fixed Schwartz
test function to be supported in the image of a sufficiently small compact
subset of $V$ under the source's diffeomorphism
$(u,r)\mapsto(ru,r)$ and to equal one on a smaller subset.

The proof of Bulj's main theorem now applies verbatim from this point.  Uniform
stationary phase gives
\[
 |T_\Phi^t f(tx)|\ge c_0t^{-d/2}
\]
on a fixed positive-measure open subset of $U$, for all sufficiently large
$t$.  Scaling $tx$ therefore yields, when $1<p\le2$,
\[
 \|T_\Phi^t f\|_p\ge c_1t^{d/p-d/2}.
\]
Division by the fixed $\|f\|_p$ gives the operator lower bound.  The case
$p\ge2$ follows from duality and
$(T_\Phi^t)^*=T_\Phi^{-t}$, exactly as in the source.  Enlarging constants over
a compact initial $t$-interval gives the stated form for $t\ge1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Necessary geometry of any counterexample]\label{cor:necessary}
If a phase satisfies the zero normalized liminf asked for in the source, then
every connected component of every regular spherical level is locally
projectively developable: in every central chart its projective Gauss map has
rank strictly below $d-2$ everywhere.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Otherwise Theorem~\ref{thm:curved} applies at a point of full Gauss rank.
\end{proof}

\section{A complete answer in dimension three}

\begin{theorem}[No submaximal phase in $d=3$]\label{thm:d3}
For every nonconstant smooth real degree-zero homogeneous phase on
$\R^3\setminus\{0\}$ and every $p\in(1,\infty)$,
\[
 \liminf_{t\to\infty}
 \frac{\|T_\Phi^t\|_{L^p\to L^p}}
 {t^{3|\frac12-\frac1p|}}>0.
\]
Consequently the source question has a negative answer in dimension three.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the curvature condition in Theorem~\ref{thm:curved} fails at every
regular point of $\Phi|_{S^2}$.  A regular level component is a compact smooth
curve.  In every central projective chart its second fundamental form
vanishes, so it is locally a projective line.  Lifting back to $S^2$, it is
locally an arc of a great circle.

On a connected component these local great circles agree on overlaps: a
great circle is determined by any nontrivial open arc.  Hence the component
lies in one great circle.  It is both open in that great circle (by the local
description) and closed (by compactness), so it is the whole great circle.

The range of the nonconstant function $\Phi|_{S^2}$ is a nondegenerate compact
interval.  Sard's theorem supplies two distinct regular values $c_1,c_2$ in
its interior.  Choose one component of each nonempty level.  The preceding
paragraph makes them two great circles.  Any two great circles on $S^2$
intersect, while distinct level sets are disjoint, a contradiction.  Therefore
a curved regular point exists, and Theorem~\ref{thm:curved} finishes the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Even phases in dimensions four and six}

We use the following published projective-geometric fact, attributed there to
Ishikawa--Morimoto.

\begin{theorem}[Ishikawa--Morimoto~\cite{Ishikawa}]\label{thm:IM}
Every compact developable $C^\infty$ hypersurface in $\RP^3$ or $\RP^5$ is a
projective hyperplane.
\end{theorem}

The same source explains why no unrestricted all-dimensional replacement is
available: nonsingular compact cubic developables exist in
$\RP^4,\RP^7,\RP^{13},\RP^{25}$.

\begin{theorem}[Even phases]\label{thm:even}
Let $d\in\{4,6\}$ and suppose in addition that $\Phi(-\xi)=\Phi(\xi)$.  If
$\Phi$ is nonconstant, then for every $p\in(1,\infty)$,
\[
 \liminf_{t\to\infty}
 \frac{\|T_\Phi^t\|_{L^p\to L^p}}
 {t^{d|\frac12-\frac1p|}}>0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Evenness makes $\Phi|_{S^{d-1}}$ descend through the antipodal quotient to a
smooth nonconstant function $\bar\Phi$ on $\RP^{d-1}$.  Suppose no regular
level has a curved point.  Choose two distinct regular values $c_1,c_2$ in
the interior of the range, and a connected component $M_i$ of each level.
Each $M_i$ is a compact embedded smooth hypersurface in $\RP^{d-1}$ and is
developable everywhere.  Since $d-1$ is $3$ or $5$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:IM} says that both $M_i$ are projective hyperplanes.  Two
projective hyperplanes in the same real projective space always intersect,
contradicting $M_1\subset\bar\Phi^{-1}(c_1)$ and
$M_2\subset\bar\Phi^{-1}(c_2)$.  Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:curved}.
\end{proof}

\section{A broad candidate family that cannot work}

\begin{corollary}[All nonconstant quadratic Rayleigh phases]\label{cor:quad}
Let $A$ be a real symmetric nonscalar $d\times d$ matrix and
\[
 \Phi_A(\xi)=\frac{\xi^{\mathsf T}A\xi}{|\xi|^2}.
\]
For every $d\ge2$ and $p\in(1,\infty)$, $\Phi_A$ has maximal norm growth in
the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:curved}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Choose $c$ strictly between the smallest and largest eigenvalues of $A$ and
not itself an eigenvalue.  The projective level $\Phi_A=c$ is the nonempty
quadric
\[
 \xi^{\mathsf T}(A-cI)\xi=0.
\]
The bilinear form $Q=A-cI$ is invertible and indefinite.  At a null vector
$\xi$, the tangent hyperplane is $\{v:Q(\xi,v)=0\}$.  If a tangent vector $v$
lies in the radical of the induced second fundamental form, then $Q(v,\cdot)$
annihilates that tangent hyperplane, hence is proportional to
$Q(\xi,\cdot)$.  Invertibility gives $v\in\R\xi$, which is zero in the
projective tangent space.  Thus the projective second fundamental form is
nondegenerate, and Theorem~\ref{thm:curved} applies.
\end{proof}

\section{Limitations, novelty, and human review}

The unrestricted source question remains open here for $d\ge4$.  The precise
remaining geometric obstruction is now sharp: an affirmative example must
make every regular level developable.  Compact nontrivial developables show
that this condition cannot be dismissed pointwise.  One must either rule out
a global foliation by such levels or build one and then prove a multiplier
upper bound.  Degenerate stationary phase alone is not an upper bound and can
increase oscillatory contributions.

No priority claim is made.  The bordered-Hessian calculation and the
dimension-three argument appear elementary but should be checked by a
harmonic analyst and a projective geometer.  The even $d=4,6$ result depends
on applying Theorem~\ref{thm:IM} to embedded regular-level components after
descent to projective space; this is why the evenness hypothesis is retained.
The literature search found no later paper claiming these consequences of
the source method.
