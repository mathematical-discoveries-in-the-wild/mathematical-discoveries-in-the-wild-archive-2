\textbf{Status.} \texttt{literature\_already\_answered}.  This is a
literature-identification note, not a claim of a new proof.

\section*{The source questions}

Haddad, Jim\'enez, and Villa, \emph{Centroids of sections of convex bodies
and Lusternik--Schnirelmann category}, arXiv:2511.22393, state two classical
questions.  If $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ is a convex body, let $\mu(K)$ be the
number of hyperplanes $H$ through its centroid $c(K)$ for which
\[
  c(K\cap H)=c(K),
\]
and let $\mu(n)=\min_K\mu(K)$.

Gr\"unbaum asks whether every $K$ has at least $n+1$ such hyperplanes.
L\"owner asks for the exact value of $\mu(n)$.  The source records
$\mu(2)=3$ and $\mu(n)=1$ for $n\geq5$, and explicitly leaves dimensions
$n=3,4$ open.

\section*{The exact later answer}

Ge and Yang, \emph{A complete solution to questions of Gr\"unbaum and
Loewner}, arXiv:2606.19865, explicitly cite arXiv:2511.22393 as reference
[11].  Their Problems 1 and 2 restate the same two questions, and their
Theorem 1.1 proves
\[
  \mu(n)=1\qquad\text{for every }n\geq3.
\]
Combining this with the known planar value gives the full answer
\[
  \boxed{\mu(2)=3,\qquad \mu(n)=1\ \text{for every }n\geq3.}
\]
Consequently, Gr\"unbaum's proposed $n+1$ lower bound holds in the plane and
fails in every dimension at least three.

The supporting construction is stronger than a bare counterexample.  For
each $n\geq3$ it produces a $C^\infty$, strictly convex body with positive
Gauss curvature having exactly one qualifying hyperplane.  The proof starts
from a small smooth radial perturbation of the Euclidean ball.  It prescribes
the critical-point pattern of the half-space-volume functional using the
hemispherical transform and an odd Morse function, then translates the body
to its centroid while preserving that pattern by a $C^2$ stability estimate.

\section*{Verification and scope}

The match is direct rather than inferential: the supporting paper names both
questions, proves the exact missing dimensions, and cites the source paper.
The source and supporting PDFs are preserved beside this note.

The source also mentions two different open directions.  It asks whether in
dimension $n\geq4$ every body has \emph{some} interior point that is the
centroid of $n+1$ sections, and it separately states the Barker--Larman
section-volume uniqueness problem.  Theorem 1.1 of Ge--Yang concerns
sections through $c(K)$ and does not settle either of those additional
questions; no claim about them is made here.
