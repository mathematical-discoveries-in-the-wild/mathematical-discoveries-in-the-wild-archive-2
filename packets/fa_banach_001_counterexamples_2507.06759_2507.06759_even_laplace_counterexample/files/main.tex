\section{Source question}

Fradelizi, Langharst, Liu, Mar\'in Sola, and Tang~\cite{FLLMT} prove a
one-dimensional Gaussian-form Gr\"unbaum inequality for
$\gamma$-transport-concave measures.  If $B=(a,b)$ has mass $t>0$ and
conditional barycenter
\[
 g_B=\frac{\int_a^b x\,d\mu(x)}{\mu(B)},
\]
their Corollary 5.2 gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proposed}
 \mu((a,g_B])\ge
 \PhiG\!\left(-\frac{I_\gamma(t)}{t}\right),
 \qquad
 I_\gamma(t)=\phig(\PhiG^{-1}(t)),
\end{equation}
where $\PhiG$ and $\phig$ are the standard Gaussian distribution function
and density.  On page 30 they ask whether the same conclusion might hold for
measures that are even.  Figure~\ref{fig:source} records the exact passage.

\section{Proof intuition}

On the negative half-line the centered Laplace density is exponential.  Its
conditional mean lies one scale length from the origin, leaving exactly an
$e^{-1}$ fraction of the half-line mass farther into the tail.  This is the
extremal one-dimensional log-concave Gr\"unbaum behavior, and it is smaller
than the stronger Gaussian expression in \eqref{eq:proposed}.  A sufficiently
long but finite interval retains the strict gap.  Taking $(-5,0)$ makes the
comparison elementary: the two sides can be separated at the rational number
$1/5$.

\section{Counterexample}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $\mu$ be the centered Laplace probability measure on $\R$,
\[
 d\mu(x)=\frac12 e^{-|x|}\,dx,
\]
and let $B=(-5,0)$.  Then $\mu$ is even, log-concave, and a convex measure in
the sense of~\cite{FLLMT}.  If $t=\mu(B)$ and $g=g_B$, then
\[
 \mu((-5,g])<\frac15<
 \PhiG\!\left(-\frac{I_\gamma(t)}{t}\right).
\]
Consequently, the proposed extension of \eqref{eq:proposed} to even measures
is false, even within the class of even log-concave convex measures.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The density is even, and
\[
 \log\!\left(\tfrac12e^{-|x|}\right)=-\log2-|x|
\]
is concave.  Its reciprocal $2e^{|x|}$ is convex, so $\mu$ is also a convex
measure under the source definition.

On $(-5,0)$ the density is $e^x/2$.  Direct integration gives
\begin{align}
 t&=\frac{1-e^{-5}}2,\label{eq:t}\\
 g&=\frac{\frac12\int_{-5}^{0}xe^x\,dx}{t}
   =-1+\frac{5}{e^5-1},\label{eq:g}\\
 \mu((-5,g])&=\frac{e^g-e^{-5}}2.\label{eq:left}
\end{align}
In particular $-1<g<0$, so the conditional barycenter lies in $B$.

We first bound \eqref{eq:left}.  From
$e>1+1+\frac12+\frac16=\frac83$ we obtain $e^5>101$.  Hence
\[
 g=-1+\frac5{e^5-1}< -1+\frac5{100}=-\frac{19}{20}.
\]
The exponential series also gives
\[
 e^{19/20}>
 1+\frac{19}{20}+\frac12\left(\frac{19}{20}\right)^2
 +\frac16\left(\frac{19}{20}\right)^3
 =\frac{122119}{48000}>\frac52.
\]
Therefore $e^g<e^{-19/20}<2/5$, and \eqref{eq:left} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leftbound}
 \mu((-5,g])<\frac{e^g}{2}<\frac15.
\end{equation}

It remains to bound the Gaussian side.  Put
$x=I_\gamma(t)/t$.  Since $I_\gamma(t)\le\phig(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi}<2/5$
and, by $e^5>101$, equation \eqref{eq:t} gives $t>50/101$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xbound}
 x<\frac{2/5}{50/101}=\frac{101}{125}=:c.
\end{equation}
Here we used the elementary Archimedean estimate $\pi>25/8$.

For $y\ge0$ one has $e^{-y}\le1-y+y^2/2$ (differentiate the
difference twice).  Thus
\[
 e^{-u^2/2}\le1-\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{u^4}{8}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
 c-\frac{c^3}{6}+\frac{c^5}{40}
 =\frac{2668545614003}{3662109375000}<\frac34.
\]
Using monotonicity of $\PhiG$ and \eqref{eq:xbound},
\begin{align*}
 \PhiG(-x)&>\PhiG(-c)\\
 &=\frac12-\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}
     \int_0^c e^{-u^2/2}\,du\\
 &>\frac12-\frac25
   \left(c-\frac{c^3}{6}+\frac{c^5}{40}\right)
 >\frac12-\frac25\cdot\frac34=\frac15.
\end{align*}
Together with \eqref{eq:leftbound}, this proves the strict reverse of
\eqref{eq:proposed}.
\end{proof}

\section{Smooth positive counterexamples}

The example is already positive and continuous, but its density is not
differentiable at the origin.  This is inessential.  Let
$\mu_\varepsilon=\mu*\gamma_\varepsilon$, where
$\gamma_\varepsilon$ is a centered Gaussian of variance $\varepsilon^2$.
The density of $\mu_\varepsilon$ is positive, smooth, even, and log-concave;
log-concavity follows from the Pr\'ekopa--Leindler theorem~\cite{Prekopa}.
A positive log-concave density has a convex reciprocal, so these are convex
measures as well.

As $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, the densities converge to the Laplace density
in $L^1$.  On the fixed bounded interval $B$, both its mass and its truncated
first moment converge.  Hence $g_{B,\varepsilon}\to g_B$, and the mass of
$(-5,g_{B,\varepsilon}]$ converges to the left side of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
The Gaussian expression is continuous in $t\in(0,1)$.  The strict gap in the
theorem therefore persists for every sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$.

\section{Verification, scope, and novelty}

The accompanying script evaluates the closed formulas at 80 decimal digits
and checks the rational polynomial bound; it is a regression test, not part
of the proof.  A bounded search through 11 August 2026 covered the run
indexes, exact arXiv id and title, the exact source phrase, current arXiv v2,
publication metadata, and combinations of \emph{even measure},
\emph{transport concavity}, \emph{Laplace}, and \emph{Gr\"unbaum}.  No later
answer or matching counterexample was found.

This packet refutes the proposed extension under evenness, including the
stronger even log-concave/convex-measure variant.  It does not weaken the
source's proved theorem under $\gamma$-transport concavity; indeed, the source
proves that an even $\gamma$-transport-concave density must be Gaussian, while
the Laplace density is not Gaussian.
