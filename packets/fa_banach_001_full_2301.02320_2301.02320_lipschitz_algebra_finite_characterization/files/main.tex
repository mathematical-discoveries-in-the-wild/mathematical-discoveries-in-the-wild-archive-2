\section{Source question}

Kania and Ma\'slany introduce a unital Banach $*$-algebra property they call
\emph{approximability by jointly non-degenerate products}: for all $f,g$ and
$\eps>0$, there are jointly non-degenerate $f',g'$ within $\eps$ such that
$f'g'=fg$ \cite{kania-maslany}.  Their Question~1 on printed page 21 asks:
\begin{quote}
``What about algebras of Lipschitz functions on zero-dimensional compact
spaces?''
\end{quote}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/open_problem_crop.png}
\end{center}

We answer this for the standard big Lipschitz algebra on a compact metric
space, and simultaneously for its H\"older variants.

\section{Definitions and proof intuition}

Let $(X,d)$ be compact metric and $0<\alpha\le1$.  Write
\[
\Lip_\alpha(X)=
\left\{f:X\to\C:
\seminorm f:=\sup_{x\ne y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)^\alpha}<\infty\right\},
\qquad
\|f\|_\alpha=\|f\|_\infty+\seminorm f.
\]
This is a unital Banach $*$-algebra under pointwise operations.  A pair
$f,g$ is \emph{jointly non-degenerate} when
$|f|^2+|g|^2$ is invertible.  In particular, such a pair has no common zero.

\paragraph{Proof intuition.}
At any accumulation point $o$, choose points $x_n\to o$ on geometrically
shrinking scales.  Give two functions alternating radial H\"older slopes:
one has value $d(x_n,o)^\alpha$ on even indices and zero on odd indices, and
the other has the reverse pattern.  McShane extension realizes this data on
all of $X$.  Any nearby exact factorization of their product by a jointly
non-degenerate pair must select one nonzero factor at $o$.  That factor stays
nonzero on a tail, forcing the other factor to vanish on that tail; one parity
then retains a H\"older error quotient exactly equal to one.

The same alternating slopes obstruct open multiplication.  A nonzero constant
perturbation of the product would force both factors to be nonzero at $o$.
The even and odd scales instead produce two mutually contracting inequalities
for those two base values, forcing both to vanish.

\section{Complete classification}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $(X,d)$ be a compact metric space and let $0<\alpha\le1$.  The following
are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $X$ is finite;
\item $\Lip_\alpha(X)$ is approximable by jointly non-degenerate products;
\item pointwise multiplication
\[
m:\Lip_\alpha(X)\times\Lip_\alpha(X)\longrightarrow\Lip_\alpha(X),
\qquad m(f,g)=fg,
\]
is open.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, every infinite zero-dimensional compact metric space gives a
negative answer to the Lipschitz-algebra part of Question~1.
\end{theorem}

We first isolate the alternating construction.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:alternating}
If $X$ is infinite, there are $o,x_1,x_2,\ldots\in X$, positive numbers
$r_n=d(x_n,o)\downarrow0$, and real $F,G\in\Lip_\alpha(X)$ such that
$r_{n+1}\le r_n/3$ and, writing $\rho_n=r_n^\alpha$,
\[
F(o)=G(o)=0,
\qquad
(F(x_n),G(x_n))=
\begin{cases}
(\rho_n,0),&n\text{ even},\\
(0,\rho_n),&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
An infinite compact metric space has an accumulation point $o$ and distinct
points tending to it.  Passing to a subsequence gives
$r_{n+1}\le r_n/3$.  On the closed subset
$S=\{o,x_1,x_2,\ldots\}$, prescribe the displayed values.  If $n<m$, then
\[
d(x_n,x_m)\ge r_n-r_m\ge\frac23r_n.
\]
The change of either prescribed function between $x_n$ and $x_m$ is at most
$r_n^\alpha$, while its change between $x_n$ and $o$ is at most
$r_n^\alpha$.  Thus each is Lipschitz on the metric space $(S,d^\alpha)$,
with constant at most $(3/2)^\alpha$.  Since $d^\alpha$ is a metric, the real
McShane extension theorem extends both functions to $X$ without increasing
that constant \cite{mcshane}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}: failure of (2) on infinite $X$]
Take $F,G$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:alternating}.  Suppose that
$f,g\in\Lip_\alpha(X)$ satisfy
\[
fg=FG,
\qquad
\|f-F\|_\alpha<\frac12,
\qquad
\|g-G\|_\alpha<\frac12,
\]
and are jointly non-degenerate.  Since $F(o)G(o)=0$, we have
$f(o)g(o)=0$; joint non-degeneracy says that exactly one of $f(o),g(o)$ is
zero.

Suppose first that $f(o)\ne0$, so $g(o)=0$.  Continuity gives
$f(x_n)\ne0$ for every sufficiently large $n$.  Since
$f(x_n)g(x_n)=F(x_n)G(x_n)=0$, it follows that $g(x_n)=0$ on this tail.  For
odd $n$ in the tail,
\[
\frac{|(g-G)(x_n)-(g-G)(o)|}{d(x_n,o)^\alpha}
=\frac{\rho_n}{r_n^\alpha}=1,
\]
contradicting $\seminorm{g-G}<1/2$.  If $g(o)\ne0$, the even subsequence gives
the identical contradiction for $f-F$.  Therefore (2) fails.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}: failure of (3) on infinite $X$]
Again take $F,G$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:alternating}, and fix
$0<\eps<1/2$.  We prove that no nonzero constant perturbation $FG+t$ can be
factored as $ab$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:near}
\|a-F\|_\alpha<\eps,
\qquad
\|b-G\|_\alpha<\eps.
\end{equation}
Set $a_0=a(o)$ and $b_0=b(o)$.  If such a factorization existed, then
$a_0b_0=t$.  Define
\[
u_n=(a-F)(x_n)-(a-F)(o),
\qquad
v_n=(b-G)(x_n)-(b-G)(o).
\]
By \eqref{eq:near}, $|u_n|,|v_n|\le\eps\rho_n$.

For even $n$,
$a(x_n)=a_0+\rho_n+u_n$ and $b(x_n)=b_0+v_n$.  Since both $FG(x_n)$ and
$FG(o)$ vanish, subtracting $a_0b_0=t$ from
$a(x_n)b(x_n)=t$ and dividing by $\rho_n$ gives
\[
0=b_0+a_0\frac{v_n}{\rho_n}
  +b_0\frac{u_n}{\rho_n}
  +(\rho_n+u_n)\frac{v_n}{\rho_n}.
\]
Letting even $n\to\infty$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b0}
(1-\eps)|b_0|\le\eps|a_0|.
\end{equation}
The odd subsequence, with the roles reversed, yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a0}
(1-\eps)|a_0|\le\eps|b_0|.
\end{equation}
As $\eps<1/2$, \eqref{eq:b0}--\eqref{eq:a0} force
$a_0=b_0=0$, contradicting $a_0b_0=t\ne0$.

If multiplication were open at $(F,G)$, the image of the product of the two
$\eps$-balls would contain a ball about $FG$.  But constants $t\ne0$ have
$\|t\mathbf1\|_\alpha=|t|$ and can be arbitrarily small, contradicting the
preceding paragraph.  Thus multiplication is not open.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}: finite $X$]
Suppose $X$ is finite.  Then $\Lip_\alpha(X)=\C^X$ algebraically and its
H\"older norm is equivalent to the supremum norm.  Given $f,g$ and a desired
accuracy, leave both unchanged except at coordinates where $f(x)=g(x)=0$;
there, replace $f(x)$ by a sufficiently small fixed nonzero scalar and keep
$g(x)=0$.  The product is unchanged, the resulting pair has no common zero
and hence is jointly non-degenerate, and finite-dimensional norm equivalence
makes the perturbation arbitrarily small.  This proves (2).

For (3), scalar multiplication $\C^2\to\C$ is open: away from $(0,0)$ this
follows from a nonzero partial derivative, and at $(0,0)$ it follows by taking
square roots.  Coordinatewise multiplication on $\C^X$ is a finite product of
these open maps, hence is open.  Equivalent finite-dimensional norms do not
change openness.
\end{proof}

\section{Scope, verification, and novelty}

The theorem includes the ordinary big Lipschitz algebra at $\alpha=1$ and is
unchanged if the standard max norm replaces the sum norm.  The alternating
functions have small-scale H\"older quotients equal to one, so they need not
belong to the little-Lipschitz subalgebra; no claim is made for that distinct
space.  A Lipschitz algebra also requires a metric, so nonmetrizable compacta
without specified compatible metric data are outside the statement.

The included script checks the alternating construction on 80-level explicit
models for five H\"older orders and checks the contraction coefficient in the
non-openness proof.  It returned \texttt{PASS}; the proof is exact and does not
depend on computation.

Bounded searches on 11 August 2026 used the exact Question~1 wording, the
paper title and authors, and combinations of \emph{jointly non-degenerate
products}, \emph{Lip(X)}, \emph{H\"older algebra}, and \emph{open
multiplication}.  They found the source, its published version, talks
reproducing the question, and general Lipschitz-algebra background, but no
later answer or this finite-space classification.  This is bounded evidence,
not exhaustive novelty certification.

Check the subsequence/McShane step, the forced-zero tail argument, and the
