trace conjecture of Paulin, Mackey, and Tropp~\cite{source,published}.

\section{The conjecture}

For a Hermitian matrix $X$, write $X_+=\max\{X,0\}$ and
$X_-=\max\{-X,0\}$ by functional calculus. The polynomial assertion in
Conjecture~7.3 of the source says that, for all Hermitian $A,B,C$, every
positive integer $q$, and every $s>0$,
\begin{align}
 \tr\!\left[C(A^q-B^q)\right]
 \leq \frac q2\tr\!\Big[&
 \big(s(A-B)_+^2+s^{-1}C_+^2\big)|A|^{q-1}\notag\\[-2mm]
 &+\big(s(A-B)_-^2+s^{-1}C_-^2\big)|B|^{q-1}\Big].
 \label{eq:conjecture}
\end{align}
The technical report omits the trace symbol on the second right side, a
typographical issue corrected in the published restatement~\cite{published}.
The distinction disappears for the scalar counterexample below.

\section{An invertible odd-power counterexample}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
Inequality~\eqref{eq:conjecture} is false already in dimension one, with the
odd exponent $q=3$. In particular, take
\[
 A=\frac1{100},\qquad B=-1,\qquad C=1,\qquad s=1.
\]
All three scalar matrices are invertible. The conjectured left side equals
$1000001/1000000$, while the right side equals $60603/200000000$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $A-B=101/100>0$ and $C=1>0$,
\[
 (A-B)_+=\frac{101}{100},\quad (A-B)_-=0,
 \quad C_+=1,\quad C_-=0.
\]
The left side of~\eqref{eq:conjecture} is
\[
 C(A^3-B^3)=\frac1{100^3}-(-1)^3
 =\frac{1000001}{1000000}.
\]
Only the $|A|^2$ term survives on the right, so that side is
\[
 \frac32\left[\left(\frac{101^2}{100^2}+1\right)\frac1{100^2}\right]
 =\frac{60603}{200000000}.
\]
Hence the asserted inequality would require
$1.000001\leq0.000303015$, which is false.
\end{proof}

The failure is strict by more than three orders of magnitude. Continuity of
both sides shows that it persists on an open neighborhood of this data; it is
not caused by a zero eigenvalue or an endpoint convention. The choice $q=3$
also lies in the odd-power family $q=2p-1$ used in matrix moment arguments.

\section{Failure for every power above one}

The same sign-allocation problem yields a uniform family.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:allq}
For every integer $q\geq2$, inequality~\eqref{eq:conjecture} has a scalar
counterexample. Take
\[
 A=0,\qquad B=-1,\qquad C=(-1)^{q+1},\qquad s=q.
\]
The left side is $1$. The right side is $0$ when $q$ is odd and $1/2$ when
$q$ is even.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Here $A-B=1$, so $(A-B)_+=1$ and $(A-B)_-=0$, while
\[
 C(A^q-B^q)=(-1)^{q+1}\big(-(-1)^q\big)=1.
\]
Because $q-1\geq1$, the entire first summand on the right of
\eqref{eq:conjecture} vanishes after multiplication by $|A|^{q-1}=0$.
If $q$ is odd, then $C=1$ and the second summand also vanishes. If $q$ is
even, then $C=-1$, so the second summand is
$s^{-1}C_-^2|B|^{q-1}=1/q$; multiplication by $q/2$ gives $1/2$.
Both cases contradict the conjectured bound.
\end{proof}

\section{Scope and verification}

Conjecture~7.3 asserts both an exponential inequality and the polynomial
inequality~\eqref{eq:conjecture}. Theorem~\ref{thm:counterexample} gives a
complete negative answer to the polynomial assertion and therefore disproves
the conjunction as stated. It makes no claim about the separate exponential
inequality. Nor does it conflict with the useful \emph{unsigned} polynomial
mean-value trace inequality proved elsewhere in the source, whose symmetric
right side avoids this sign-allocation obstruction.

The included exact-arithmetic script verifies the displayed fractions and the
family in Proposition~\ref{prop:allq}. A bounded search on 13 August 2026 used
the exact conjecture name and formula. It found the source, the 2016 published
restatement, and later unsigned variants, but no earlier proof or disproof of
the signed polynomial assertion. The novelty verdict is therefore
\emph{apparently new within the bounded search}, not exhaustive.
