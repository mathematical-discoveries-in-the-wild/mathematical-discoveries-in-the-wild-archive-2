\section{Source problem and result}

For $k,d\ge2$, let $\Cbar(k,d)$ be the largest size, over all real
$d$-dimensional Banach spaces $X$, of a family of unit vectors
$x_1,\ldots,x_m\in X$ such that
\[
 \left\|\sum_{i\in I}x_i\right\|\le1
 \qquad\text{for every }I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\},\quad |I|=k.
\]
Swanepoel's Theorem~26 gives $\Cbar(k,6)=k+1$ for $k\ge17$ and
$\Cbar(k,7)=k+1$ for $k\ge41$. Conjecture~28 predicts the same conclusion
whenever $k\ge2d-1$.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/open_problem_crop.png}
\end{center}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The two endpoint values immediately below the source's displayed ranges are
\[
 \boxed{\Cbar(16,6)=17},\qquad
 \boxed{\Cbar(40,7)=41}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof keeps the integer jump position in the source's scalar optimization.
At precisely these parameters the resulting Frobenius bound forces equality
in the rank inequality, and its equality structure is impossible.

\section{A sharp scalar lemma at the two endpoints}

Call $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m)$ \emph{$k$-collapsing} if
$|\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i|\le1$ for every $k$-subset $I$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:scalar}
Suppose $k=m-2$, $\alpha_m=1$, and $\alpha$ is $k$-collapsing.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $m=18$, then $\sum_{j=1}^{18}\alpha_j^2\le3$. Equality holds,
up to permuting the first seventeen coordinates, only for
\[
 (\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{17})
 = (\underbrace{1/2,\ldots,1/2}_{5},
    \underbrace{-1/4,\ldots,-1/4}_{12})
\]
or
\[
 (\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{17})
 = (\underbrace{3/7,\ldots,3/7}_{6},
    \underbrace{-2/7,\ldots,-2/7}_{11}).
\]
The corresponding complete row sums are $1/2$ and $3/7$.
\item If $m=42$, then $\sum_{j=1}^{42}\alpha_j^2\le6$. Equality holds,
up to permutation, only when the first forty-one coordinates consist of
thirteen copies of $1/2$ and twenty-eight copies of $-1/4$. The complete row
sum is $1/2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First note the elementary normalization $|\alpha_j|\le1$ for $j\ne m$.
Indeed, comparing two $k$-subsets differing only by $m$ and $j$ gives
$\alpha_j\ge-1$. There are at most $k-1$ positive coordinates, since a
$k$-subset of positive coordinates containing $\alpha_m=1$ would have sum
greater than one. As $m-k=2$, choose two nonpositive coordinates other than
$j,m$ and compare a $k$-subset containing $j,m$ with one in which those two
coordinates replace $j,m$. The upper and lower subset bounds give
$\alpha_j\le1$.

Write the remaining coordinates in decreasing order
$x_1\ge\cdots\ge x_{m-1}$. A $k$-subset either includes the distinguished
entry $1$ and omits two of the $x_j$, or excludes $1$ and omits one $x_j$.
Using $|x_j|\le1$ and the ordering, all the subset inequalities are equivalent
to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polytope}
 x_1+\cdots+x_{m-3}\le0,
 \qquad x_2+\cdots+x_{m-1}\ge-1.
\end{equation}
For example, the two displayed inequalities are the upper bound for the
largest sum including $1$ and the lower bound for the smallest sum excluding
$1$. They imply the other two extreme bounds because $x_{m-2}\le0$ and
$x_2\le1$.

We maximize the strictly convex function $Q(x)=\sum_jx_j^2$ over the
polytope given by the ordering and \eqref{eq:polytope}. An elementary
active-constraint calculation lists its vertices. If all coordinates agree,
they are either all zero or all $-1/(m-2)$. Otherwise there is one jump: for
some $1\le t\le m-3$, the first $t$ coordinates equal
\[
 a_t=\frac{m-3-t}{t+m-3},
 \qquad
 b_t=\frac{-t}{t+m-3}
\]
and the remaining $m-1-t$ coordinates equal $b_t$. The last vertex is
$(0,\ldots,0,-1)$. This list follows because at a nonconstant vertex both
inequalities in \eqref{eq:polytope} are active and all but one of the ordering
inequalities are active. Conversely, the displayed points meet the constraints.

At a one-jump vertex the off-diagonal square sum is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q}
 q_m(t)=t a_t^2+(m-1-t)b_t^2
 =\frac{t\bigl((m-3)^2-(m-5)t\bigr)}{(t+m-3)^2}.
\end{equation}
As a function of real $t$, its derivative has the sign of
\[
 (m-3)^2-(3m-13)t.
\]
Thus it is unimodal, with real maximizer $(m-3)^2/(3m-13)$.

For $m=18$ this maximizer is $225/41\in(5,6)$. Direct substitution gives
$q_{18}(5)=q_{18}(6)=2$, while unimodality and the remaining vertices show
that these are the only maxima. The formulas for $a_t,b_t$ give the two
stated rows and their sums. For $m=42$ the real maximizer is
$1521/113\in(13,14)$; here
\[
 q_{42}(13)=5,\qquad q_{42}(14)=\frac{14042}{2809}<5.
\]
Hence $t=13$ is the unique integer maximizer, giving the stated row. Adding
the distinguished square $\alpha_m^2=1$ proves both bounds and equality
statements.
\end{proof}

\section{Rank equality rules out both families}

We use the sharp trace--Frobenius inequality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rank}
 |\operatorname{tr}A|^2\le \rank(A)\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|^2.
\end{equation}
For a real matrix, equality holds exactly when $A$ is symmetric and all its
nonzero eigenvalues are equal. For completeness, this follows by applying
Schur triangularization, Cauchy--Schwarz to the nonzero eigenvalues, and
unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm. Equality makes the Schur form
diagonal with equal real nonzero diagonal entries.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Suppose first that eighteen unit vectors in a six-dimensional Banach space are
16-collapsing. Choose a norming functional $f_i$ for each $x_i$ and form
\[
 A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{18},\qquad a_{ij}=f_i(x_j).
\]
Then $\rank A\le6$, $a_{ii}=1$, and every row is a scalar 16-collapsing
family. Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar} and \eqref{eq:rank} give
\[
 6\ge\rank A\ge\frac{18^2}{18\cdot3}=6.
\]
Every inequality is therefore an equality. In particular, $A$ is symmetric,
has rank six, and all its nonzero eigenvalues equal
$\operatorname{tr}A/6=3$. Every row is one of the two equality types in
Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar}.

The types cannot mix. Indeed, an off-diagonal entry shared by rows of different
types would have to belong simultaneously to
$\{1/2,-1/4\}$ and $\{3/7,-2/7\}$, which are disjoint. Hence all rows have
the same type, so the all-ones vector is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue
equal to the common row sum, either $1/2$ or $3/7$. This contradicts the fact
that every nonzero eigenvalue is $3$. Thus no such eighteen-vector family
exists.

For forty-two unit vectors in dimension seven that are 40-collapsing, the
same construction and the second part of Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar} yield
\[
 7\ge\rank A\ge\frac{42^2}{42\cdot6}=7.
\]
Equality makes $A$ symmetric of rank seven with every nonzero eigenvalue equal
to $42/7=6$. Every row has the unique equality type and row sum $1/2$, so the
all-ones vector is instead an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1/2$, again a
contradiction.

Any larger family contains a subfamily of the forbidden size, while preserving
the fixed $k$-subset condition. Therefore $\Cbar(16,6)\le17$ and
$\Cbar(40,7)\le41$. Conversely, in a two-dimensional subspace take $k+1$
unit vectors summing to zero (for example, a Euclidean regular $(k+1)$-gon).
Every $k$-sum is the negative of the omitted unit vector. Hence
$\Cbar(k,d)\ge k+1$, proving both equalities.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification, scope, and novelty}

Exact-rational enumeration independently checks the two maxima, equality
positions, row sums, and rank thresholds. It is included only as a sanity
check. The neighboring cases $(d,k)=(6,15)$ and $(7,39)$ merely force the
rank to be at least the ambient dimension, not equality, so the spectral
contradiction does not extend. A scan of the same endpoint formula through
$m<2000$ found no other exact rank-equality hits. The full Conjecture~28
remains open.

A bounded search through 11 August 2026 used the exact title and terminology
and the citation graph of DOI \texttt{10.1090/tran/6601}. It found a survey
chapter and an adjacent signed-subset-sum paper, but no source stating either
endpoint value. Priority is not asserted.
