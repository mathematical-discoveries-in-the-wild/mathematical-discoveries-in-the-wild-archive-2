\section{The source question}

For $n\geq2$, let
\[
 \SOS(n)=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^r g_j^2:g_j\in
 \R[x_1,\ldots,x_n],\ r<\infty\right\}
\]
and let $\POS(n)$ be the cone of polynomials nonnegative on $\R^n$.
Borcea asks whether every $p\in\POS(n)$ can be represented as $T(f)$, where
$f\in\SOS(n)$ and $T$ is a linear partial differential operator with
constant coefficients satisfying $T(\SOS(n))\subseteq\POS(n)$
\cite[p.~11]{Borcea}.  The original question is reproduced in
Figure~\ref{fig:question}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/open_question_crop.png}
\caption{The final question on page 11 of arXiv:0811.4374v3.  The source uses
$SOS(n)$ and $\overline{POS}(n)$ for the cones denoted here by $\SOS(n)$ and
$\POS(n)$.}
\label{fig:question}
\end{figure}

The answer is no in every dimension under consideration.  In fact, the
possible outputs admit a complete description.

\section{Exact reachability classification}

As in the source, an operator with constant coefficients may have infinite
order:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T}
 T=\sum_{\gamma\in\Nzero^n}q_\gamma\,\partial^\gamma,
 \qquad q_\gamma\in\R.
\end{equation}
The sum is nevertheless finite on each polynomial.  Define
\[
 \Reach_n=\{T(f): f\in\SOS(n),\ T\text{ is as in \eqref{eq:T}},\
 T(\SOS(n))\subseteq\POS(n)\}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[automatic SOS preservation]\label{thm:auto}
If a constant-coefficient operator $T$ satisfies
$T(\SOS(n))\subseteq\POS(n)$, then in fact
\[
 T(\SOS(n))\subseteq\SOS(n).
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \boxed{\Reach_n=\SOS(n)}
\]
for every $n\geq1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $d\geq0$ and index rows and columns by
$A_d=\{\alpha\in\Nzero^n:|\alpha|\leq d\}$.  Form the finite Hankel matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
 H_d=\bigl(h_{\alpha\beta}\bigr)_{\alpha,\beta\in A_d},
 \qquad
 h_{\alpha\beta}=(\alpha+\beta)!\,q_{\alpha+\beta}.
\end{equation}
For arbitrary real coefficients $c_\alpha$, put
$g(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in A_d}c_\alpha x^\alpha$.  Since $g^2\in\SOS(n)$,
the hypothesis and evaluation at the origin give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psd}
 0\leq T(g^2)(0)
 =\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in A_d}
 c_\alpha c_\beta(\alpha+\beta)!q_{\alpha+\beta}
 =c^{\mathsf T}H_dc.
\end{equation}
Thus $H_d$ is positive semidefinite.  This is precisely the
constant-coefficient specialization of the Hankel condition in Theorem 4.4
of the source, but \eqref{eq:psd} also proves it directly.

Now let $g$ be any polynomial of degree at most $d$, and set
\[
 v_\alpha(x)=\frac{\partial^\alpha g(x)}{\alpha!}
 \qquad(\alpha\in A_d).
\]
The multivariate Leibniz rule yields the polynomial identity
\begin{align}
 T(g^2)(x)
 &=\sum_{\gamma}q_\gamma
   \sum_{\alpha+\beta=\gamma}
   \frac{\gamma!}{\alpha!\beta!}
   \partial^\alpha g(x)\partial^\beta g(x) \notag\\
 &=v(x)^{\mathsf T}H_dv(x).                       \label{eq:gram}
\end{align}
Choose a real Gram factorization $H_d=B^{\mathsf T}B$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:gram} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicit-sos}
 T(g^2)(x)=\sum_k\left(
   \sum_{\alpha\in A_d}B_{k\alpha}
   \frac{\partial^\alpha g(x)}{\alpha!}
 \right)^2,
\end{equation}
an explicit sum of polynomial squares.  Applying \eqref{eq:explicit-sos}
to every summand of $f=\sum_jg_j^2$ proves
$T(f)\in\SOS(n)$.

It follows that $\Reach_n\subseteq\SOS(n)$.  The reverse inclusion is
immediate: for $p\in\SOS(n)$, take $f=p$ and $T$ to be the identity
operator.  The identity has constant coefficients and maps $\SOS(n)$ into
$\POS(n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[GNS interpretation]
Equation \eqref{eq:H} defines a positive semidefinite polynomial moment
functional $L(x^\gamma)=\gamma!q_\gamma$.  Then
$T(g^2)(x)=L_t(g(x+t)^2)$.  Only finitely many classes of monomials occur for
a fixed $g$; choosing an orthonormal basis in their finite-dimensional GNS
span again turns the squared Hilbert-space norm into the finite SOS
decomposition \eqref{eq:explicit-sos}.  Thus the conclusion does not require
the multivariate moment functional to possess a representing measure.
\end{remark}

\section{An explicit counterexample in every dimension}

Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m}
 m(x,y)=x^4y^2+x^2y^4+1-3x^2y^2.
\end{equation}
The arithmetic--geometric mean inequality gives
\[
 x^4y^2+x^2y^4+1
 \geq3\sqrt[3]{x^6y^6}=3x^2y^2,
\]
so $m\geq0$ on $\R^2$.  We include the short standard proof that $m$ is
not SOS, making the counterexample self-contained.

\begin{lemma}[Newton-polytope support restriction]\label{lem:newton}
If a real polynomial $P$ is a sum of squares $P=\sum_jQ_j^2$, then
\[
 \operatorname{Newt}(Q_j)\subseteq\tfrac12\operatorname{Newt}(P)
 \qquad\text{for every }j.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If an exponent in some $Q_j$ lay outside the half polytope, a separating
linear weight would make twice its weight strictly larger than the maximal
weight in $P$.  But the highest-weight part of $\sum_jQ_j^2$ is the sum of
squares of the highest-weight parts of the $Q_j$ and therefore cannot cancel.
This contradicts the definition of the Newton polytope of $P$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:motzkin}
The polynomial $m$ in \eqref{eq:m} does not belong to $\SOS(2)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $m=\sum_jg_j^2$, then $\deg g_j\leq3$.  Homogenizing each $g_j$ to
degree three would express the Motzkin form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M(x,y,z)=x^4y^2+x^2y^4+z^6-3x^2y^2z^2
\end{equation}
as a sum of squares of cubic forms.

The Newton polytope of $M$ is the triangle with vertices
$(4,2,0)$, $(2,4,0)$, and $(0,0,6)$; the exponent $(2,2,2)$ is its
barycenter.  The lattice points in half this triangle are exactly
\[
 (2,1,0),\qquad(1,2,0),\qquad(0,0,3),\qquad(1,1,1).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:newton} therefore forces every cubic summand to have the form
\[
 Q_j=a_jx^2y+b_jxy^2+c_jz^3+d_jxyz.
\]
In $\sum_jQ_j^2$, the coefficient of $x^2y^2z^2$ is then
$\sum_jd_j^2\geq0$: no product of two distinct displayed monomials has that
exponent.  In \eqref{eq:M} the coefficient is $-3$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[full negative answer]\label{cor:negative}
For every $n\geq2$, the answer to Borcea's final question is negative.
Explicitly, view $m(x_1,x_2)$ as a polynomial in
$\R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$.  It belongs to $\POS(n)$, but there are no
$f\in\SOS(n)$ and no constant-coefficient $T$ with
$T(\SOS(n))\subseteq\POS(n)$ such that $T(f)=m$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:auto} would force $m=T(f)$ to be SOS in $n$ variables.
Setting $x_3=\cdots=x_n=0$ in such a decomposition would make $m$ SOS in
two variables, contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:motzkin}.
\end{proof}

\section{Scope, verification, and novelty}

The classification covers the source's full operator class, including
infinite-order constant-coefficient operators.  No moment-measure theorem is
used; finite positive-semidefinite Hankel matrices and their Gram
factorizations suffice.  The argument also isolates why allowing a
nonnegative output does not enlarge the image cone: positivity on all input
squares already supplies the same Gram matrix needed for an output SOS.

The Leibniz/Hankel identity, the half-Newton-polytope lattice points, and the
Motzkin coefficient contradiction were independently checked by the included
symbolic verifier.  A second derivation through the finite-dimensional GNS
construction and audits of infinite order, $n=2$, and dimension embedding are
recorded in the accompanying attempt note.

Novelty is provisional.  Searches through 13 August 2026 covered the exact
question, the source title and DOI, constant-coefficient SOS-preserver and
Hankel/Gram variants, the local run corpus, and the four works in the source's
OpenAlex citation record.  The later positivity-preserver treatments
arXiv:0902.0279 and arXiv:2407.15654 were inspected directly.  They discuss
nonnegativity preservation and related heat-flow SOS phenomena, but the
searches found no statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:auto}, the reachability
classification, or an answer to the displayed source question.  Priority is
therefore not claimed.  Human review should focus first on the index matching
in \eqref{eq:gram}; after that, the result is finite-dimensional linear
algebra.
