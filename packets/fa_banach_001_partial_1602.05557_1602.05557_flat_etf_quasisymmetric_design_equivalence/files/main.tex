\section*{Target and result}

Problem 2 of Fickus--Mixon--Jasper \cite{FMJ} asks, for even $q$, whether
there are $q(q^2+q-1)$ vectors with entries in $\{\pm1\}$ that form an ETF
for $\one^\perp\subset\mathbb R^{q(q+1)}$ and have pairwise inner products of
absolute value $q$.  Their first open case is $q=6$.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.84\textwidth]{figures/problem_2_q6_crop.png}
\end{center}

We give a constructive equivalence with one sharply specified object from
design theory.

\begin{theorem}
Let $q$ be even and set
\[
 m=q(q+1),\qquad v=m-1=q^2+q-1,\qquad b=qv.
\]
The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item There is a matrix $\Phi\in\{\pm1\}^{m\times b}$ whose columns form an
ETF for $\one^\perp\subset\mathbb R^m$ and whose distinct column inner
products have absolute value $q$.
\item There is a quasi-symmetric design on $v$ points with $b$ blocks and
parameters
\[
  k=\frac{q(q+1)}2,\quad
  r=\frac{q^2(q+1)}2,\quad
  \lambda=\frac{q^2(q+1)}4,
\]
whose two block-intersection numbers are
\[
 x_1=\frac{q^2}{4},\qquad x_2=\frac{q(q+2)}4.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Both transformations are explicit and inverse up to row/column switching.
\end{theorem}

For $q=6$, Problem 2 is therefore equivalent to a quasi-symmetric
$2$-$(41,21,63)$ design with $246$ blocks, replication $126$, and block
intersections $9$ and $12$.

\section*{Proof: ETF to design}

Assume (1).  Since the $b$ columns have squared norm $m$ and form a tight
frame for the $v=m-1$ dimensional space $\one^\perp$, their frame operator is
\[
 \Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T}=\frac{bm}{v}
 \left(I-\frac1mJ\right)=q^2(q+1)I-qJ.                 \tag{1}
\]
Multiply each column by its first entry.  This makes the first row all $+1$
and preserves every hypothesis.  For a column $j$, let $B_j$ be the set of
indices among the remaining $v$ rows where its entry is $-1$.

Every column is orthogonal to $\one$, so
\[
 |B_j|=\frac m2=k.                                      \tag{2}
\]
Every other row has inner product $-q$ with the first row by (1), hence has
\[
 r=\frac{b+q}{2}=\frac{q^2(q+1)}2                       \tag{3}
\]
negative entries.  Any two such rows also have inner product $-q$; their
negative supports therefore have symmetric difference $(b+q)/2=r$ and
intersection
\[
 \lambda=r-\frac r2=\frac{q^2(q+1)}4.                  \tag{4}
\]
Thus the $B_j$ form a $2$-$(v,k,\lambda)$ design with replication $r$.

If $t=|B_i\cap B_j|$, the corresponding column inner product is
\[
 1+v-4(k-t)=4t-m.
\]
Its absolute value is $q$, so $t=(m-q)/4=q^2/4$ or
$t=(m+q)/4=q(q+2)/4$.  This proves (2).

\section*{Proof: design to ETF}

Conversely, let $N$ be the $v\times b$ incidence matrix of the design and set
\[
 \Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\one^{\mathsf T}\\ J-2N\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Equation (2) makes every column sum equal to $m-2k=0$.  Two columns whose
blocks meet in $t$ points have inner product $4t-m$, hence $\pm q$.

The first row has inner product $b-2r=-q$ with every other row.  Two distinct
rows of $N$ meet in $\lambda$ blocks, so their signed versions have inner
product
\[
 b-4(r-\lambda)=-q.
\]
All row norms are $b$, and consequently
\[
 \Phi\Phi^{\mathsf T}=(b+q)I-qJ
 =q^2(q+1)\left(I-\frac1mJ\right).                     \tag{5}
\]
Thus $\Phi$ has rank $m-1$ and is tight precisely on $\one^\perp$.
Its column coherence is $q/m=1/(q+1)$, while
\[
 \frac{b-(m-1)}{(m-1)(b-1)}=\frac1{(q+1)^2};
\]
so this is the Welch value.  The columns are an ETF, proving (1). \qed

\section*{Block graph and the first open case}

Let $A$ join two blocks when they meet in $x_2$ points.  From
\[
 N^{\mathsf T}N=(k-x_1)I+(x_2-x_1)A+x_1J,
 \qquad NN^{\mathsf T}=(r-\lambda)I+\lambda J,
\]
one obtains a strongly regular block graph with degree
\[
 K=\frac{(q-1)(q+1)(q+2)}2,
\]
restricted eigenvalues $(q^2-2)/2$ and $-(q+2)/2$, and parameters
\[
 \Lambda=\frac{(q+4)(q^2-2)}4,\qquad
 M=\frac{q^2(q+2)}4.
\]
For $q=6$ this is $\operatorname{SRG}(246,140,85,72)$ with restricted
eigenvalues $17,-4$.  The standard integrality, absolute, complement, and
Krein conditions all pass.  Moreover, recent general restrictions on
quasi-symmetric designs \cite{Bagchi} depend here on invariants of the actual
unknown graph.  The equivalence therefore sharpens the finite target but does
not settle its existence.

\section*{Proof intuition}

The extra flatness in Problem 2 is decisive.  After one harmless column
switch, every vector is the signed incidence vector of a half-size block.
ETF tightness controls row intersections, producing a $2$-design; ETF
equiangularity controls column intersections, making that design
quasi-symmetric.  Conversely those two intersection laws reproduce the entire
row and column Gram data of the ETF.  The hard $q=6$ question is now a single
finite incidence problem rather than a continuous frame search.
