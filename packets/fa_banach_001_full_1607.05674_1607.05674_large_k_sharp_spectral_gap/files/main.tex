\section{Question and answer}

Embed $\U(k)$ in $\U(n)$ as $a\mapsto a\oplus I_{n-k}$, let
$m_{\U(k)}$ be its Haar probability, and define Pisier's central probability
\[
 \mu_{k,n}=\int_{\U(n)}\delta_s*m_{\U(k)}*\delta_{s^{-1}}\,dm_{\U(n)}(s).
\]
Let $\sigma_n$ be the defining representation and
$S_n=\{\sigma_n,\overline{\sigma_n}\}$.  If $\ell=n-k$, then
\[
 \widehat\mu_{k,n}(\sigma_n)
 =\widehat\mu_{k,n}(\overline{\sigma_n})=\frac{\ell}{n}I.
\]
Remark 2.11 of arXiv:1607.05674v7 asks whether a comparable spectral gap
holds when $k=[\theta n]$ and $1/2<\theta<1$.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth,trim=40 230 40 320,clip]
 {figures/source_question_page_17.png}
\end{center}
\medskip

\begin{theorem}[sharp large-block gap]\label{thm:main}
Let $1\leq \ell=n-k\leq k$.  Then $\mu_{k,n}$ has a
$(\delta_{k,n},\gamma_{k,n})$-spectral gap with respect to $S_n$, where
\[
 \delta_{k,n}=\frac{\ell}{n},\qquad
 \gamma_{k,n}=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{n(n+1)}.
\]
Moreover, the second constant is exact:
\[
 \sup_{\substack{\pi\in\widehat{\U(n)},\ \pi\ne 1\\
                   \pi\notin S_n}}
 \|\widehat\mu_{k,n}(\pi)\|
 =\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{n(n+1)}.
\]
The maximum is attained by $\Sym^2(\sigma_n)$ and its conjugate.
\end{theorem}

Since $(\ell+1)/(n+1)<1$, one has $\gamma_{k,n}<\delta_{k,n}$.
For $k=[\theta n]$ with $1/2<\theta<1$,
\[
 \delta_{k,n}\longrightarrow 1-\theta,
 \qquad
 \gamma_{k,n}\longrightarrow(1-\theta)^2.
\]
Thus the answer is affirmative with exactly the square asymptotics requested
in the source.

\section{The source reduction}

We use the notation and formulas of Sections 2.6--2.8 of the source.  An
irreducible representation is indexed by
$m=(m_1\geq\cdots\geq m_n)\in\mathbb Z^n$.  If $m_n\leq0$, put
\[
 d=-m_n,\qquad \lambda_i=m_i+d,
\]
so $\lambda$ is a partition with $\lambda_n=0$, and let
$[d]^k=(d,\ldots,d,0,\ldots,0)$, with $d$ repeated $k$ times.  The source's
Lemma 2.5 gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source-fourier}
 \widehat\mu_{k,n}(\pi_m)
 =\frac{s_{\lambda/[d]^k}(1^\ell)}{s_\lambda(1^n)}I
\end{equation}
when $[d]^k\subset\lambda$, and zero otherwise.  It is also zero if
$m_n>0$.  Hence all coefficients in question are nonnegative scalars.

The decisive literal observation is that part (ii) of the source's Lemma 2.8
assumes
\[
 n-k\leq k,
\]
exactly the range later called unknown.  Remark 2.11 instead says the lemmas
apply when $k\leq n-k$.  Even the source's four displayed bounds in part
(ii) are strictly below $\ell/n$, so they already yield some spectral gap in
the purportedly unknown range.  We now make the strongest of those estimates
sharp.

Put
\[
 A=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{n(n+1)},\qquad
 B=\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{n(n-1)}.
\]
The cases in Lemma 2.8 give the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $d=0$, then every off-target nonzero coefficient is at most $A$
  when $\lambda_1\geq2$, and at most $B$ when $\lambda_1=1$.
\item If $d\geq1$ and $\lambda_k\geq2$, the coefficient is at most $A$.
\item If $d=1$, $\lambda_k=1$, and $\lambda_1=1$ but
  $\lambda\ne(1^{n-1},0)$, it is at most $B$.
\item If $d=1$, $\lambda_k=1$, $\lambda_1\geq2$, and
  $\lambda_{n-1}=0$, it is at most $B$.  The source assumes $k<n-1$ in
  this line; when $k=n-1$ the case is impossible because
  $\lambda_k=\lambda_{n-1}=1$.
\end{itemize}
Since $B\leq A$, only the remaining mixed case needs attention.

\section{The sharp mixed-case estimate}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mixed}
Assume $\ell\leq k$, $d=1$, $\lambda_k=1$, $\lambda_1\geq2$, and
$\lambda_{n-1}\geq1$.  Then
\[
 \frac{s_{\lambda/[1]^k}(1^\ell)}{s_\lambda(1^n)}
 \leq \frac{\ell^2}{n^2-1}\leq A.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Monotonicity of $\lambda$ and $\lambda_k=1$ show that
$\lambda_k=\cdots=\lambda_{n-1}=1$.  For $i<n$, put
$\alpha_i=\lambda_i-1$.  In the skew diagram $\lambda/[1]^k$, row $k$ is
empty.  Above it lies the diagram $\alpha$, shifted one column to the right;
below it lies a column of length $\ell-1$.  The components are disjoint, so
the skew Schur function factors:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor}
 s_{\lambda/[1]^k}(1^\ell)
 =s_\alpha(1^\ell)s_{(1^{\ell-1})}(1^\ell)
 =\ell\,s_\alpha(1^\ell).
\end{equation}
If $s_\alpha(1^\ell)=0$, the conclusion is immediate.  Otherwise $\alpha$
has at most $\ell$ nonzero rows; pad it to
$\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_\ell)$.

Weyl's dimension product, with the factors inside the first $\ell$ rows
cancelled against $s_\alpha(1^\ell)$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E}
 \frac{s_\lambda(1^n)}{s_\alpha(1^\ell)}
 =k\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}
 \left[
   \prod_{j=\ell+1}^{n-1}
   \frac{\alpha_i+j-i}{j-i}
 \right]
 \frac{\alpha_i+n-i+1}{n-i}.
\end{equation}
Every factor on the right is nondecreasing in its corresponding
$\alpha_i$.  Since $\lambda_1\geq2$, the partition $\alpha$ is nonzero, so
the product is minimized at $\alpha=(1,0,\ldots,0)$.  At that point the
three telescoping products give
\[
 \frac{s_\lambda(1^n)}{s_\alpha(1^\ell)}
 \geq
 k\cdot\frac{n-1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{n+1}{n-1}
       \cdot\frac{n-1}{k}
 =\frac{n^2-1}{\ell}.
\]
Combining this with \eqref{eq:factor} proves the first inequality.  Finally,
\[
 \frac{\ell^2}{n^2-1}
 \leq\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{n(n+1)}
\]
is equivalent to $\ell\leq n-1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the sharp theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:main}]
Formula \eqref{eq:source-fourier}, the zero cases surrounding it, and the
exhaustive Lemma 2.8 split listed above cover every irreducible
representation.  The trivial representation is omitted; the partitions
$(1,0,\ldots,0)$ with $d=0$ and $(1,\ldots,1,0)$ with $d=1$ are precisely
$\sigma_n$ and $\overline{\sigma_n}$ and have coefficient $\ell/n$.
Every other coefficient is bounded by $A$, by the source estimates and
Lemma \ref{lem:mixed}.

It remains to prove sharpness.  For $\pi=\Sym^2(\sigma_n)$, the
$\U(k)$-fixed subspace is $\Sym^2(\mathbb C^\ell)$, because
$\mathbb C^n=\mathbb C^k\oplus\mathbb C^\ell$ and $\U(k)$ acts trivially
on the second summand.  Centralization turns the fixed-space projection into
the scalar normalized rank, so
\[
 \widehat\mu_{k,n}(\pi)
 =\frac{\dim\Sym^2(\mathbb C^\ell)}
        {\dim\Sym^2(\mathbb C^n)}I
 =\frac{\binom{\ell+1}{2}}{\binom{n+1}{2}}I
 =A I.
\]
The conjugate representation gives the same value.  This proves equality in
the supremum and completes the theorem.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification and novelty boundary}

An independent exact-arithmetic script evaluates the numerator in
\eqref{eq:source-fourier} by the Jacobi--Trudi determinant and the denominator
by Weyl's formula.  Bounded partition enumeration for $2\leq n\leq9$ and all
$\lceil n/2\rceil\leq k<n$ found the exact maximum $A$ in every case.  This
check is supplementary; no finite enumeration is used in the proof.

The bounded novelty audit searched the arXiv id, exact title, source remark,
measure notation, sharp formula, and later unitary spectral-gap literature.
The four works indexed as citing the published chapter use it as Sidon/Paley
background and do not discuss Remark 2.11.  The closest later primary source
is G. Alon and D. Puder, \emph{Aldous-type Spectral Gaps in Unitary Groups},
arXiv:2603.00353.  It studies noncentral sums of projections obtained from
coordinate-block Haar measures.  Its mean-field theorem determines the
smallest eigenvalue of that noncentral Laplacian, whereas the result here
determines the normalized rank of one block-fixed subspace after averaging
over all unitary conjugates.  That paper does not cite the source or state the
formula above.  No erratum or later exact answer was located through
2026-08-11.

The conservative mathematical claim is therefore a candidate full sharp
answer pending human review.  Even if the inequality reversal in Remark 2.11
is regarded as merely clerical, the exact off-target maximum and its
disconnected-skew proof are a material strengthening not stated in the
source.
