\section{Source questions and scope}

Bernardes Jr. and Messaoudi ask whether shadowing and structural stability imply one
another (Question 13), whether an expansive structurally stable invertible operator must
be hyperbolic (Question 15), and whether hyperbolicity coincides with structural stability
relative to $GL(X)$ (Question 16) \cite{BM19}. The first source crop records Question 13;
the second records Questions 15 and 16.

The present note proves two general spectral obstructions. The first uses ordinary
structural stability and removes the spectral point $1$. The second uses the near-identity
conjugacy in \emph{strong} structural stability and removes every unimodular eigenvalue
of the adjoint. This gives a complete positive answer to Question 15 with ``structurally
stable'' replaced by ``strongly structurally stable'', on arbitrary complex Banach spaces.
For ordinary stability, it identifies the only residual-spectrum regime in which a
counterexample can remain.

\section{Definitions}

Let $X$ be a real or complex Banach space and $T\in GL(X)$. Following \cite{BM19},
$T$ is \emph{structurally stable} if some $\varepsilon>0$ has the following property:
whenever $\alpha\in\Cb$ is bounded and Lipschitz with
\[
 \|\alpha\|_\infty\leq\varepsilon,
 \qquad \Lip(\alpha)\leq\varepsilon,
\]
the homeomorphisms $T$ and $T+\alpha$ are topologically conjugate. It is
\emph{strongly structurally stable} if, for every $\gamma>0$, the tolerance can be
chosen so that a conjugacy $h$ can additionally satisfy $\|h-I\|_\infty\leq\gamma$.

For a complex Banach space, $\Torus=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}$,
$\spa(T^*)$ is the point spectrum of the adjoint, and $\saa(T)$ is the approximate
point spectrum. An invertible $T$ is hyperbolic when $\sigma(T)\cap\Torus=\varnothing$.

\section{Proof intuition}

Ordinary stability already controls constant affine perturbations. Since $T$ fixes the
origin, every conjugate map $T+a$ must have a fixed point; this says exactly that every
small $a$ belongs to $\ran(I-T)$.

Strong stability supplies much more: a conjugacy uniformly close to the identity. If
$T^*f=\lambda f$ with $|\lambda|=1$, follow under that conjugacy an orbit whose
$f$-coordinate is the circle $r\lambda^n$. A bounded Lipschitz perturbation, supported
in a thin annulus of the scalar $f$-coordinate, changes the quotient multiplier from
$\lambda$ to $(1+c)\lambda$. Closeness of the conjugacy traps the quotient orbit in the
annulus, while the new multiplier forces exponential radial growth. These two facts are
incompatible.

For an expansive ordinarily stable operator, the source paper proves uniform
expansivity \cite[Theorem~6(a)]{BM19}. Thus a nonhyperbolic unit-circle spectral point
cannot be approximate spectrum: it has closed proper range and becomes an adjoint
eigenvalue. The ordinary result removes $\lambda=1$; the strong result removes all such
$\lambda$.

\section{Main results}

\begin{theorem}[Ordinary stability forces affine solvability]\label{thm:onto}
Let $T\in GL(X)$ be structurally stable. Then $I-T$ is surjective. If, in addition,
$T$ is expansive, then $I-T$ is invertible; equivalently, $1\in\rhoop(T)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose a structural-stability tolerance $\varepsilon>0$. For any $a\in X$ with
$\|a\|\leq\varepsilon$, the constant map $\alpha_a(x)=a$ satisfies
$\|\alpha_a\|_\infty\leq\varepsilon$ and $\Lip(\alpha_a)=0$. Hence there is a
homeomorphism $h:X\to X$ such that
\[
 h\circ T=(T+\alpha_a)\circ h.
\]
Evaluating at $0$ gives
\[
 h(0)=Th(0)+a,
 \qquad\text{so}\qquad
 (I-T)h(0)=a.
\]
Thus $\ran(I-T)$ contains the ball $\varepsilon B_X$. Since it is a linear subspace,
$\ran(I-T)=X$.

If $T$ is expansive and $(I-T)x=0$, then $T^nx=x$ for every $n\in\mathbb Z$.
After normalizing a nonzero such $x$, this contradicts expansivity. Hence $I-T$ is
injective. The bounded inverse theorem now gives $(I-T)^{-1}\in\mathcal B(X)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Strong stability excludes unimodular adjoint eigenvalues]\label{thm:adjoint}
Let $X$ be a complex Banach space and let $T\in GL(X)$ be strongly structurally
stable. Then
\[
 \spa(T^*)\cap\Torus=\varnothing.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose instead that $T^*f=\lambda f$ for some $f\in X^*\setminus\{0\}$ and
$\lambda\in\Torus$. Normalize $\|f\|=1$ and choose $v\in X$ with $f(v)=1$.
Fix $\gamma>0$ and put $r=4\gamma$. Let $\rho:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]$ be a Lipschitz
function such that
\[
 \rho(t)=1\quad(t\in[r-\gamma,r+\gamma]),
 \qquad
 \rho(t)=0\quad(t\notin[r-2\gamma,r+2\gamma]).
\]
For $c>0$, define
\[
 \alpha_c(y)=c\lambda f(y)\rho(|f(y)|)v.
\]
The scalar map $z\mapsto z\rho(|z|)$ is bounded and Lipschitz. Consequently
$\|\alpha_c\|_\infty$ and $\Lip(\alpha_c)$ tend to zero with $c$. Choose $c>0$
small enough that $\alpha_c$ is admitted by strong structural stability (and, if
necessary, small enough that $T+\alpha_c$ is a homeomorphism). We obtain a
homeomorphism $h=I+u$ satisfying
\[
 h\circ T=(T+\alpha_c)\circ h,
 \qquad \|u\|_\infty\leq\gamma.
\]

Take $x=rv$ and set $q_n=f(h(T^nx))$ for $n\in\mathbb Z$. Since
$f(T^nx)=\lambda^n f(x)=\lambda^n r$,
\[
 |q_n-\lambda^nr|=|f(u(T^nx))|\leq\gamma.
\]
It follows that $|q_n|\in[r-\gamma,r+\gamma]$ for every $n$, so the cutoff is
identically one along this orbit. Applying $f$ to the conjugacy equation yields
\begin{align*}
 q_{n+1}
 &=f(Th(T^nx))+f(\alpha_c(h(T^nx)))\\
 &=\lambda q_n+c\lambda q_n
  =(1+c)\lambda q_n.
\end{align*}
Therefore $|q_n|=(1+c)^n|q_0|$ for $n\geq0$. But $|q_0|\geq r-\gamma>0$,
whereas the annulus estimate gives $|q_n|\leq r+\gamma$ for every $n$. This is a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Sharp expansive consequences]\label{cor:expansive}
Let $X$ be a complex Banach space.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $T\in GL(X)$ is expansive and structurally stable, then
      $1\in\rhoop(T)$. If $T$ is not hyperbolic, every
      $\lambda\in\sigma(T)\cap\Torus$ satisfies $\lambda\neq1$,
      $T-\lambda I$ is bounded below with closed proper range, and
      $\lambda\in\spa(T^*)$.
\item If $T\in GL(X)$ is expansive and strongly structurally stable, then $T$ is
      hyperbolic.
\item If $T$ is expansive and $\zeta T$ is structurally stable for every
      $\zeta\in\Torus$, then $T$ is hyperbolic.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first statement in (a) is Theorem~\ref{thm:onto}. Theorem 6(a) of \cite{BM19}
says that an expansive structurally stable operator is uniformly expansive. Hence
$\saa(T)\cap\Torus=\varnothing$. If
$\lambda\in\sigma(T)\cap\Torus$, the operator $T-\lambda I$ is bounded below,
so its range is closed. It is not surjective, since $\lambda\in\sigma(T)$, and therefore
Hahn--Banach gives a nonzero $f\in X^*$ annihilating its range. Thus
$T^*f=\lambda f$. The fact that $\lambda\neq1$ follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:onto}.

Part (b) follows because Theorem~\ref{thm:adjoint} rules out the adjoint eigenvalue
forced by every putative unit-circle spectral point in (a).

For (c), $\zeta T$ is expansive whenever $T$ is. Applying
Theorem~\ref{thm:onto} to $\zeta T$ shows that $I-\zeta T$ is invertible for every
$\zeta\in\Torus$. Equivalently, $\sigma(T)\cap\Torus=\varnothing$.
\end{proof}

\section{What remains open}

Theorem~\ref{thm:onto} does not answer Question 13 or the ordinary version of
Question 15. An arbitrary conjugacy supplied by ordinary structural stability need not
be close to the identity, boundedly displaced, homogeneous, or equivariant under scalar
rotations. The annular resonance in Theorem~\ref{thm:adjoint} uses the estimate
$\|h-I\|_\infty\leq\gamma$ at exactly one indispensable point.

Eight focused upgrade routes were checked: affine perturbations; transfer to $\zeta T$;
rescaling an arbitrary conjugacy; periodic/root-of-unity perturbations; difference-operator
selection for shadowing; an upgrade through topological stability; finite interpolation by
small Lipschitz homeomorphisms; and linear rank-one perturbations for the relative
$GL(X)$ question. In each failed route, the same obstruction remains: a uniformly
expansive nonhyperbolic operator can have closed proper range for $T-\lambda I$ and an
exact eigenfunctional of $T^*$, but no approximate eigenvectors of $T$ from which to
build an arbitrarily small-Lipschitz resonant orbit.

Thus a counterexample to ordinary Question 15, if one exists, must live entirely in the
nontrivial residual-spectrum regime described in Corollary~\ref{cor:expansive}(a). No
new conclusion about Question 16 is claimed.

\section{Verification and bounded novelty search}

The proof is symbolic. The main verification points are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item constant perturbations are bounded with Lipschitz constant zero, and conjugacy
      sends the fixed point $0$ of $T$ to a fixed point of $T+a$;
\item $z\mapsto z\rho(|z|)$ is globally bounded and Lipschitz because $\rho$ has
      compact support;
\item the conjugacy orientation gives $q_{n+1}=(1+c)\lambda q_n$;
\item uniform expansivity removes the approximate point spectrum from $\Torus$, so a
      remaining spectral point has closed proper range and produces an adjoint eigenvector.
\end{itemize}

The run's registry, solution, attempt, and proof-gap indexes were searched by arXiv id
and by the core phrases. A bounded web/arXiv search through 17 August 2026 checked
the source paper, Bayart's weighted-shift characterization \cite{Bayart21}, the
composition-operator work \cite{Maiuriello22}, the 2024 shadowing paper
\cite{BP24}, and the 2025 topological-stability paper \cite{BCDFP25}. The latter proves
that topological stability implies shadowing on Banach spaces, but its near-identity
semiconjugacy hypothesis is stronger than the ordinary structural stability used here.
No exact statement of Theorems~\ref{thm:onto}--\ref{thm:adjoint} or the general
strong-stability extension in Corollary~\ref{cor:expansive}(b) was found. This is not a
proof of novelty.
