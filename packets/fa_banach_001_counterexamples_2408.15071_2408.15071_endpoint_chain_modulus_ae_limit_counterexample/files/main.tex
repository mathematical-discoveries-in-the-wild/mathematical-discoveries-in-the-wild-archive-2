\begin{abstract}
Caputo--Cavallucci ask whether their almost-everywhere limit stability
proposition for weak chain upper gradients remains true for the
asymmetric endpoint integrals $\lambda=0,1$.  It does not.  On the real
line, change the a.e. limit representative at the single endpoint $0$.
For $\lambda=1$, all one-edge chains $(x,0)$ detect that change, and
their endpoint modulus is infinite because admissibility forces
$\rho(x)\ge1/|x|$.  Reversing the construction gives the same negative
answer for $\lambda=0$.
\end{abstract}

\section{The endpoint question}

Fix $p\ge1$ and $\varepsilon>0$.  For a chain
$\mathsf c=(q_0,\ldots,q_N)$, the source defines
\[
 \int_{\mathsf c}^{\lambda}g
 =\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}
   \bigl(\lambda g(q_i)+(1-\lambda)g(q_{i+1})\bigr)
   d(q_i,q_{i+1}).
\]
Thus $\lambda=1$ samples the left endpoint of each edge, while
$\lambda=0$ samples the right endpoint.  A Borel $g\ge0$ is a weak
$(\varepsilon,\lambda)$-upper gradient of $u$ when
\[
 u(q_N)-u(q_0)\le\int_{\mathsf c}^{\lambda}g
\]
outside a family of chains of $(\varepsilon,\lambda,p)$-modulus zero.

Proposition 5.9 proves, for the symmetric integral, that if
$u_j\to u$ pointwise a.e., $g_j$ are weak upper gradients of $u_j$, and
$g_j\to g$ in $L^p$, then $g$ is a weak upper gradient of $u$.  The
source says it does not know whether this remains true at $\lambda=1$
(and treats $\lambda=0$ as analogous):

\section{A modulus calculation}

Let $X=\R$ with Euclidean distance and Lebesgue measure, and put
$r=\min\{\varepsilon/2,1\}$.  For $0<|x|<r$, let
\[
 \mathsf c_x^+=(x,0),\qquad
 \mathsf c_x^-=(0,x).
\]
Both are $\varepsilon$-chains.  Define
$\Gamma_+=\{\mathsf c_x^+:0<|x|<r\}$ and
$\Gamma_-=\{\mathsf c_x^-:0<|x|<r\}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:modulus}
For every $p\ge1$,
\[
 \Mod^{\varepsilon,1}_p(\Gamma_+)=\infty,
 \qquad
 \Mod^{\varepsilon,0}_p(\Gamma_-)=\infty.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $\rho$ is admissible for $\Gamma_+$ at $\lambda=1$, then for every
$0<|x|<r$,
\[
 1\le\int_{\mathsf c_x^+}^{1}\rho=\rho(x)|x|.
\]
Hence $\rho(x)\ge |x|^{-1}$ throughout the punctured interval, and
\[
 \int_{\R}\rho^p\,dx
 \ge2\int_0^r x^{-p}\,dx=\infty.
\]
Every admissible function therefore has infinite $L^p$ cost, proving
the first equality.  For $\Gamma_-$ at $\lambda=0$ the sampled endpoint
is again $x$, so
$\int_{\mathsf c_x^-}^{0}\rho=\rho(x)|x|$ and the same proof applies.
\end{proof}

\section{Counterexamples to limit stability}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:counterexample}
The endpoint analogues of Proposition 5.9 fail on $\R$ for every
$p\ge1$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, both for $\lambda=1$ and for
$\lambda=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $\lambda=1$, take
\[
 u_j\equiv0,\qquad g_j\equiv0,
 \qquad u=\mathbf1_{\{0\}},\qquad g\equiv0.
\]
Then $u_j\to u$ pointwise Lebesgue-a.e. and $g_j\to g$ in $L^p$.
Because every $u_j$ is constant, $g_j=0$ satisfies the endpoint upper
gradient inequality on every chain.  However, for each
$\mathsf c_x^+=(x,0)\in\Gamma_+$,
\[
 u(0)-u(x)=1>0=\int_{\mathsf c_x^+}^{1}g.
\]
The whole family $\Gamma_+$ is a failure family and has infinite
modulus by Theorem~\ref{thm:modulus}; it cannot be discarded.  Thus
$g\notin\mathrm{WUG}^{\varepsilon,1}_p(u)$.

For $\lambda=0$, instead take $u=-\mathbf1_{\{0\}}$ while keeping
$u_j=g_j=g=0$.  Along every $\mathsf c_x^-=(0,x)\in\Gamma_-$,
\[
 u(x)-u(0)=1>0=\int_{\mathsf c_x^-}^{0}g.
\]
Again Theorem~\ref{thm:modulus} prevents discarding the failure family,
so $g\notin\mathrm{WUG}^{\varepsilon,0}_p(u)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Why the symmetric proof breaks}

For $0<\lambda<1$, chains meeting a measure-zero exceptional set form
a modulus-zero family, which makes a.e. convergence sufficient.  At
$\lambda=1$, the integral ignores the terminal vertex, so a null-set
change at the terminal endpoint can be detected by a family of positive
or infinite modulus; at $\lambda=0$ the same issue occurs at the initial
vertex.  A repaired endpoint statement would need convergence outside
an endpoint-exceptional set of zero endpoint modulus, rather than merely
outside a measure-zero set.
