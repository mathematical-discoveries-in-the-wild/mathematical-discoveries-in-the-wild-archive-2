\section{Source question}

The source is Karsten Kruse, \emph{Extension of vector-valued functions and
weak-strong principles for differentiable functions of finite order},
arXiv:1910.01952 \cite{Kruse}.  Theorem~6.6 proves the full finite-order
principle when $E$ is semi-Montel and $G\subset E'$ determines boundedness.
Question~6.9(i), on printed page~21, asks whether there are other target spaces
and whether the positive $C^0$ result for spaces whose bounded sets are
precompact extends to positive finite order.  The complete source question is
reproduced below.

We solve a precise Banach-space slice of the first sentence.  The result also
shows that, once the theorem permits a proper test set $G\subset E'$, the
answer depends on the pair $(E,G)$ and not only on $E$.

\section{Definitions and statement}

Let $E$ be a real or complex Banach space and let $G\subset E'$ determine
boundedness, meaning that a set $B\subset E$ is norm bounded whenever
\[
  \sup_{x\in B}|g(x)|<\infty \qquad\text{for every }g\in G.
  \tag{1}
\]
This already forces $G$ to separate points: if every $g\in G$ annihilates
$x$, then $\{nx:n\in\N\}$ is $G$-bounded, so (1) implies $x=0$.

We say that $(E,G)$ has the \emph{relative sequential Schur property} if
\[
 \SG\qquad
 \left.
 \begin{array}{c}
 (x_n)\subset E\text{ is norm bounded},\\
 g(x_n)\longrightarrow0\text{ for every }g\in G
 \end{array}
 \right\}
 \Longrightarrow \|x_n\|\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{2}
\]
Because $G$ determines boundedness, every $G$-null sequence is automatically
norm bounded.  Thus the boundedness line in (2) is logically redundant here,
but records the standard relative Schur formulation.

\begin{theorem}[Banach-pair classification]\label{thm:classification}
Let $E$ be a Banach space, let $G\subset E'$ determine boundedness, and fix
$k\in\N$.  The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] The pair $(E,G)$ has property $\SG$.
\item[(ii)] For every real Banach space $Y$, every open $U\subset Y$, and every map
      $f:U\to E$, the conditions
      \[
        g\circ f\in C^k(U,\K)\qquad(g\in G)
      \]
      imply $f\in C^k(U,E)$ in the Fr\'echet sense.
\item[(iii)] The implication in (ii) holds just for $Y=\R$ and open intervals
      $U\subset\R$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, the same relative Schur condition characterizes the principle
for every positive finite order, and one-dimensional curves already detect
failure.
\end{theorem}

The theorem is stronger in its domain than the source question, which only
requires $U\subset\R^d$.

\section{Proof intuition}

There are two short mechanisms.  First, $G$-scalar smoothness makes all the
relevant differences, difference quotients, Taylor remainders, and derivative
differences converge coordinatewise under $G$.  Condition (1) makes each such
sequence norm bounded, while $\SG$ upgrades its $G$-convergence to norm
convergence.  This constructs the derivatives and proves their continuity.

Conversely, if $\SG$ fails, take a bounded $G$-null sequence $(x_n)$ that stays
away from zero.  Put increasingly narrow smooth bumps with values in the
directions $x_n$ at points $t_n\downarrow0$.  Scale the $n$th bump by the
$k$th power of its width.  Its first $k-1$ derivatives then disappear at the
accumulation point.  The $k$th derivative still takes the value $x_n$ at the
bump centers, so it is not norm continuous; after applying any fixed $g\in G$,
those values become $g(x_n)\to0$, so every scalarization is nevertheless
$C^k$.

\section{Proof of the classification}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:classification}]
The implication (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii) is immediate.  We first prove
(i)$\Rightarrow$(ii), and then build a counterexample proving
(iii)$\Rightarrow$(i).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: the case $k=1$.}
Suppose $\SG$ holds and $g\circ f\in C^1(U)$ for every $g\in G$.  If
$y_n\to y$ in $U$, then
\[
  g(f(y_n)-f(y))\longrightarrow0\qquad(g\in G).
\]
This difference sequence is norm bounded by (1), and hence converges to zero
in norm by $\SG$.  Since $U$ and $E$ are metrizable, $f$ is continuous.

Fix $y\in U$ and $h\in Y$.  For nonzero $t$ sufficiently close to zero put
\[
  q_t(h)=\frac{f(y+th)-f(y)}{t}.
\]
If these quotients were not norm Cauchy as $t\to0$, there would be
$s_n,t_n\to0$ and $\varepsilon>0$ such that
$\|q_{s_n}(h)-q_{t_n}(h)\|\geq\varepsilon$.  But for every $g\in G$,
\[
  g(q_{s_n}(h)-q_{t_n}(h))\longrightarrow0
\]
because $g\circ f$ is differentiable at $y$.  Boundedness determination and
$\SG$ give a contradiction.  Completeness of $E$ therefore gives a limit
\[
  A_yh:=\lim_{t\to0}q_t(h)\in E,
  \qquad
  g(A_yh)=D(g\circ f)(y)h.
  \tag{3}
\]
Since $G$ separates points, (3) makes $A_y$ linear.  Moreover,
\[
 \sup_{\|h\|\leq1}|g(A_yh)|=\|D(g\circ f)(y)\|<\infty
 \qquad(g\in G).
\]
Thus $A_y$ maps the unit ball of $Y$ to a $G$-bounded set, and (1) shows that
$A_y\in\Lop(Y,E)$.

We next show that $A_y$ is the Fr\'echet derivative.  If not, there are
$h_n\to0$ with $h_n\ne0$ and $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
 z_n:=\frac{f(y+h_n)-f(y)-A_yh_n}{\|h_n\|},
 \qquad \|z_n\|\geq\varepsilon.
 \tag{4}
\]
Equation (3) and scalar Fr\'echet differentiability give
$g(z_n)\to0$ for every $g\in G$.  Again (1) and $\SG$ contradict (4).  Hence
$Df(y)=A_y$.

Finally $Df$ is operator-norm continuous.  Otherwise, for some $y_n\to y$ we
could choose $\|h_n\|=1$ and $\varepsilon>0$ with
\[
 \|(Df(y_n)-Df(y))h_n\|\geq\varepsilon.
\]
For each $g\in G$ the left-hand vectors are $G$-null, since
\[
 |g((Df(y_n)-Df(y))h_n)|
 \leq\|D(g\circ f)(y_n)-D(g\circ f)(y)\|\longrightarrow0.
\]
This contradicts $\SG$.  We have proved $f\in C^1(U,E)$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: induction on the order.}
Assume the assertion has been proved through order $m-1$, where $m\geq2$,
and suppose every $g\circ f$ is $C^m$.  By induction $f\in C^{m-1}$.
Write
\[
 F(y)=D^{m-1}f(y)\in\Lop^{m-1}(Y,E).
\]
For fixed $y$ and $v_1,\ldots,v_m\in Y$, apply the quotient argument from
Step~1 to
\[
 \frac{[F(y+tv_1)-F(y)](v_2,\ldots,v_m)}{t}.
\]
It converges in $E$; call its limit
$B_y(v_1,\ldots,v_m)$.  Scalarization gives
\[
 g(B_y(v_1,\ldots,v_m))
   =D^m(g\circ f)(y)(v_1,\ldots,v_m).
 \tag{5}
\]
Separation by $G$ makes $B_y$ $m$-linear.  For each $g$, (5) is bounded on
the product of the unit balls by $\|D^m(g\circ f)(y)\|$.  Thus (1) makes the
whole image norm bounded, and $B_y\in\Lop^m(Y,E)$.

The map $F$ is Fr\'echet differentiable at $y$ with derivative
$h\mapsto B_y(h,\cdot)$.  Indeed, failure in operator norm would give
$h_n\to0$ and unit vectors $v_{2,n},\ldots,v_{m,n}$ such that the $E$-valued
normalized remainders
\[
 \frac{[F(y+h_n)-F(y)-B_y(h_n,\cdot)]
 (v_{2,n},\ldots,v_{m,n})}{\|h_n\|}
 \tag{6}
\]
stay away from zero.  Applying $g$ bounds (6) by the operator norm of the
normalized Fr\'echet remainder of $D^{m-1}(g\circ f)$, which tends to zero.
Property $\SG$ again contradicts the assumed lower bound.

The same unit-vector argument proves continuity of $y\mapsto B_y$ in
$\Lop^m(Y,E)$: if it failed along $y_n\to y$, evaluate
$B_{y_n}-B_y$ on unit $m$-tuples witnessing the failure and use continuity
of every scalar $D^m(g\circ f)$.  Therefore $F\in C^1$ and
$f\in C^m$.  Induction proves (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) for the fixed $k$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: a smooth-bump converse.}
Suppose $\SG$ fails.  After passing to a subsequence, there are a bounded
sequence $(x_n)\subset E$ and $\delta>0$ such that
\[
 g(x_n)\longrightarrow0\quad(g\in G),
 \qquad \|x_n\|\geq\delta\quad(n\in\N).
 \tag{7}
\]
Choose $\phi\in C_c^\infty((-1,1))$ with $\phi^{(k)}(0)=1$.  Let
\[
 t_n=2^{-n},\qquad w_n=2^{-3n},\qquad
 I_n=(t_n-w_n,t_n+w_n).
\]
The intervals $I_n$ are pairwise disjoint and accumulate only at zero.  Set
\[
 f(t)=
 \begin{cases}
  w_n^k\,\phi\!\left(\dfrac{t-t_n}{w_n}\right)x_n,&t\in I_n,\\[6pt]
  0,&t\notin\bigcup_n I_n.
 \end{cases}
 \tag{8}
\]
Compact support of $\phi$ makes (8) smooth across every endpoint of $I_n$.
For $t\in I_n$ and $0\leq j\leq k$,
\[
 f^{(j)}(t)=w_n^{k-j}
 \phi^{(j)}\!\left(\frac{t-t_n}{w_n}\right)x_n.
 \tag{9}
\]
For $0\leq j\leq k-1$, boundedness of $(x_n)$ and (9) give
\[
 \frac{\|f^{(j)}(t)\|}{|t|}
 \leq C_j\frac{w_n^{k-j}}{t_n-w_n}
 \leq 2C_j\frac{w_n}{t_n}
 =2C_j2^{-2n}\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{10}
\]
Starting with $j=0$ and proceeding successively, (10) proves that all
derivatives through order $k$ exist at zero and equal zero; (9) also gives
continuity there through order $k-1$.

For any fixed $g\in G$, the scalar $k$th derivative on $I_n$ is
\[
 (g\circ f)^{(k)}(t)=
 \phi^{(k)}\!\left(\frac{t-t_n}{w_n}\right)g(x_n).
\]
Its supremum on $I_n$ tends to zero by (7).  Thus
$g\circ f\in C^k(\R)$ for every $g\in G$.  On the other hand,
\[
 f^{(k)}(t_n)=x_n,
 \qquad f^{(k)}(0)=0,
\]
so (7) says that $f^{(k)}$ is not norm continuous at zero.  Consequently
$f\notin C^k(\R,E)$, contradicting (iii).  Hence (iii) implies $\SG$ and the
proof is complete.
\end{proof}

\section{Consequences and sharpness}

\begin{corollary}[Full dual]\label{cor:full-dual}
For a Banach space $E$ and any fixed $k\geq1$, every weakly $C^k$ map from
an open subset of any Banach space into $E$ is $C^k$ if and only if $E$ has
the Schur property.
\end{corollary}

For $G=E'$, condition $\SG$ is exactly the ordinary Schur property.  The
positive implication, including arbitrary Banach domains and all finite
orders, is Corollaries~2.2--2.3 of Bachir--Lancien \cite{BachirLancien};
their Proposition~2.4 also gives a differentiability-level converse.  Thus
Corollary~\ref{cor:full-dual} is not claimed as new.

Taking $E=\ell_1$ and $G=E'=\ell_\infty$ gives an infinite-dimensional
affirmative example for the first sentence of Question~6.9(i) in the
full-dual Banach setting.  It is not semi-Montel: an infinite-dimensional
Banach space has non-precompact closed unit ball.

The dependence on $G$ is sharp even for that same $E$.  Regard
$c_0\subset\ell_\infty=(\ell_1)'$.  The set $c_0$ determines boundedness of
$\ell_1$: if $B\subset\ell_1$ is pointwise bounded on $c_0$, view its members
as continuous linear functionals on the Banach space $c_0$ and apply uniform
boundedness.  But the unit vectors $(e_n)$ satisfy
\[
 \langle e_n,a\rangle=a_n\longrightarrow0\qquad(a\in c_0),
 \qquad \|e_n\|_1=1.
\]
Hence $(\ell_1,c_0)$ fails $\SG$, and (8) gives an explicit weak--strong
counterexample for every finite $k\geq1$.  Therefore no classification that
mentions only the Banach target $E$ can describe all admissible proper test
sets $G$.

\section{Literature and novelty bounds}

Bachir--Lancien's 2003 paper \cite{BachirLancien} proves the full-dual Schur
case and is the essential closest result.  The argument above deliberately
credits that mechanism; its candidate advance is the exact equivalence for
an arbitrary boundedness-determining pair $(E,G)$, the reduction to curves,
and the sharp contrast between $(\ell_1,\ell_\infty)$ and
$(\ell_1,c_0)$ in the framework of Kruse's question.

On 9 August 2026, bounded searches of the run indexes, the local full-source
arXiv corpus, and official-arXiv/web results used the source title and the
terms ``weakly $C^k$,'' ``weak--strong principle,'' ``Schur property,''
``$\tau$-Schur,'' and ``$\sigma(E,G)$-Schur.''  They recovered the 2003
full-dual theorem and literature on relative Schur topologies, but no source
stating the differentiability classification for boundedness-determining
$G$.  This is a limited novelty audit, not publication-level certification.

\section{Scope and human review}

Theorem~\ref{thm:classification} is a complete Banach-pair result and a
substantial partial answer to Question~6.9(i).  It does not settle the
locally convex precompact-bounded-set question, Questions~6.9(ii)--(iv), or
the possible classification of Banach spaces for which the principle holds
simultaneously for every $G$ that determines boundedness.

Human review should focus on the higher-order operator-norm induction, the
interpretation of the source question when $G$ varies, and whether the
relative extension has appeared under $\tau$-Schur terminology.  The bump
construction and the $c_0$ boundedness-determination example are also worth
checking independently.
