\begin{abstract}
Gupta and Verma ask for a nontrivial necessary-and-sufficient condition for
commutativity of the natural quaternion convolution of Akila and Roopkumar.
We give a complete classification.  Two quaternion-valued functions commute
under this convolution exactly when the imaginary parts of their quaternion
Fourier transforms are collinear at every frequency.  Equivalently, the two
Fourier values lie pointwise in a common complex slice of the quaternion
algebra.  We prove the equivalence from the convolution theorem and Fourier
injectivity, derive three explicit real component identities and two
time-domain convolution identities, and exhibit broad families beyond the
sufficient cases recorded in the survey.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question and the answer}

Fix quaternion units $i,j,k$ with $ij=k$ and write every quaternion-valued
function uniquely as
\[
                 f=f_1+jf_2,\qquad f_1,f_2:\R\to\C_i,
\]
where $\C_i=\R+i\R$.  For a complex function $h$, put
$\check h(x)=h(-x)$.  The natural convolution from
Akila--Roopkumar \cite{AR}, in the notation of the source survey, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:natural}
\begin{split}
(f\ostar g)(x)
={}&(f_1*g_1)(x)-(\check{\overline{f_2}}*g_2)(x)\\
 &+j\big((\check{\overline{f_1}}*g_2)(x)+(f_2*g_1)(x)\big).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The quaternion Fourier transform is componentwise,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qft}
                 \widehat f(\xi)=\widehat f_1(\xi)+j\widehat f_2(\xi),
\end{equation}
and its decisive compatibility with \eqref{eq:natural} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convthm}
                 \widehat{f\ostar g}(\xi)=\widehat f(\xi)\widehat g(\xi).
\end{equation}

The survey notes that $\ostar$ is generally noncommutative and asks for a
nontrivial necessary-and-sufficient condition for
$f\ostar g=g\ostar f$ \cite[p.~26]{GV}.  Its final sentence prints an ordinary
star, but the immediately preceding definition, convolution theorem, and
known sufficient cases unambiguously identify \eqref{eq:natural} as the
intended operation.  Figure~\ref{fig:source} reproduces the source passage.

The theorem is stated on $L^1_{\Hq}(\R)$, where every term in
\eqref{eq:natural} lies in $L^1$ and the Fourier transform is pointwise
defined and injective.  It therefore also applies on the common
$L^1\cap L^2$ domain in the source's $L^2$ formulation.

\begin{theorem}[complete commutativity criterion]\label{thm:main}
Let $f,g\in L^1_{\Hq}(\R)$.  The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $f\ostar g=g\ostar f$ almost everywhere.
\item $\widehat f(\xi)\widehat g(\xi)
      =\widehat g(\xi)\widehat f(\xi)$ for every $\xi\in\R$.
\item
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross}
 \Imq\widehat f(\xi)\mathbin{\times}\Imq\widehat g(\xi)=0
 \qquad(\xi\in\R).
\end{equation}
where pure imaginary quaternions are identified with $\R^3$ in the
basis $(i,j,k)$.
\item At each frequency $\xi$, the two values $\widehat f(\xi)$ and
$\widehat g(\xi)$ belong to a common complex slice
$\C_{u(\xi)}=\R+\R u(\xi)$ for some pure unit quaternion $u(\xi)$,
with arbitrary $u(\xi)$ allowed when both values are real.
\end{enumerate}

More explicitly, write
\[
 \widehat f=a+jb,\qquad \widehat g=c+jd,
\]
with $a=a_0+ia_1$, $b=b_0+ib_1$, $c=c_0+ic_1$, and
$d=d_0+id_1$.  Then the equivalent pointwise scalar criterion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:three}
\boxed{
 b_1d_0-b_0d_1=0,\qquad
 a_1d_1-b_1c_1=0,\qquad
 a_1d_0-b_0c_1=0.}
\end{equation}
All eight real functions in \eqref{eq:three} are evaluated at the same
frequency $\xi$, and the equations must hold for every $\xi$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Proof}

We isolate the elementary quaternion fact that makes the criterion exact.

\begin{proposition}[centralizers in $\Hq$]\label{prop:quaternion}
For $p=p_0+p_1i+p_2j+p_3k$ and
$q=q_0+q_1i+q_2j+q_3k$, put
$\mathbf p=(p_1,p_2,p_3)$ and $\mathbf q=(q_1,q_2,q_3)$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:commutator}
                pq-qp=2\,\mathbf p\times\mathbf q.
\end{equation}
Consequently, $p$ and $q$ commute if and only if their imaginary vectors are
collinear.  Equivalently, they lie in one complex slice $\R+\R u$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The standard scalar--vector multiplication rule is
\[
(p_0+\mathbf p)(q_0+\mathbf q)
=(p_0q_0-\mathbf p\!\cdot\!\mathbf q)
 +(p_0\mathbf q+q_0\mathbf p+\mathbf p\times\mathbf q).
\]
Interchanging $p$ and $q$ preserves every term except the cross product,
which changes sign, proving \eqref{eq:commutator}.  A vanishing cross product
is precisely collinearity.  If a nonzero imaginary vector occurs, normalize
its common direction to obtain $u$; if both vanish, every $u$ works.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Apply the convolution theorem \eqref{eq:convthm} to both orders:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bothorders}
 \widehat{f\ostar g}=\widehat f\,\widehat g,
 \qquad
 \widehat{g\ostar f}=\widehat g\,\widehat f.
\end{equation}
If the convolutions agree, their Fourier transforms agree, so (i) implies
(ii).  Conversely, (ii) and \eqref{eq:convthm} give equal quaternion Fourier
transforms.  Componentwise injectivity of the complex Fourier transform on
$L^1(\R)$ yields equality of the two quaternion-valued convolutions almost
everywhere.  Thus (i) and (ii) are equivalent.

At each fixed $\xi$, Proposition~\ref{prop:quaternion} identifies (ii) with
(iii), and also identifies (iii) with (iv).  It remains only to expand (iii).
Because
\[
a+jb=a_0+a_1i+b_0j-b_1k,
\]
the two imaginary vectors are
\[
       (a_1,b_0,-b_1),\qquad(c_1,d_0,-d_1).
\]
Their cross product is
\[
 (b_1d_0-b_0d_1,\;a_1d_1-b_1c_1,\;a_1d_0-b_0c_1),
\]
so its vanishing is exactly \eqref{eq:three}.
\end{proof}

There is a useful second algebra check.  Since $jz=(\overline z)j$ for
$z\in\C_i$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairs}
(a+jb)(c+jd)=ac-(\overline b)d
             +j((\overline a)d+bc).
\end{equation}
Subtracting the reverse product in \eqref{eq:pairs} gives the equivalent pair
of complex equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complexcriterion}
 (\overline d)b-(\overline b)d=0,
 \qquad
 (\overline a)d-da+bc-(\overline c)b=0.
\end{equation}
This independently reproduces all three real equations in
\eqref{eq:three}.

\section{Time-domain form and genuinely new examples}

Direct subtraction in \eqref{eq:natural}, using commutativity of ordinary
complex convolution, gives an exact time-domain version.

\begin{corollary}[time-domain criterion]\label{cor:time}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, $f\ostar g=g\ostar f$ if and
only if
\begin{align}
 \check{\overline{f_2}}*g_2
   &=\check{\overline{g_2}}*f_2,                       \label{eq:time1}\\
 \check{\overline{f_1}}*g_2+f_2*g_1
   &=\check{\overline{g_1}}*f_2+g_2*f_1.              \label{eq:time2}
\end{align}
\end{corollary}

The frequency criterion is more structural: it says that the slice may vary
with $\xi$, and it automatically handles frequencies at which one transform
is real or zero.  In particular, it strictly exceeds the two sufficient
cases stated in the survey.

\begin{corollary}[a nontrivial commuting family]\label{cor:example}
Fix a pure unit quaternion $u$, and let $\phi,\psi\in L^1(\R)$ be real even
functions.  For arbitrary real constants $r,s,t,v$, the functions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:family}
             f=(r+su)\phi,\qquad g=(t+vu)\psi.
\end{equation}
commute under $\ostar$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The Fourier transforms $\widehat\phi$ and $\widehat\psi$ are real-valued.
Hence the Fourier values of both functions lie in the fixed commutative slice
$\C_u$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:main} applies.
\end{proof}

For example, take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:example}
 u=\frac{i+j}{\sqrt2},\qquad
 f=(1+u)e^{-x^2},\qquad g=(2+3u)e^{-2x^2}.
\end{equation}
Neither function is $\C_i$-valued and neither is real-valued, yet
$f\ostar g=g\ostar f$.  Thus this is not covered by either easy case in the
survey.  More generally, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} allows the common slice
$\C_{u(\xi)}$ to rotate with frequency, subject only to the Fourier transforms
coming from the chosen function class.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
The open problem has an exact answer: natural-convolution commutativity is
neither an ad hoc parity condition nor global membership in one fixed copy of
$\C$.  It is precisely pointwise collinearity of the two imaginary Fourier
vectors.  Conditions \eqref{eq:three}, \eqref{eq:complexcriterion}, and
\eqref{eq:time1}--\eqref{eq:time2} are respectively real-frequency,
complex-frequency, and time-domain versions of the same classification.
