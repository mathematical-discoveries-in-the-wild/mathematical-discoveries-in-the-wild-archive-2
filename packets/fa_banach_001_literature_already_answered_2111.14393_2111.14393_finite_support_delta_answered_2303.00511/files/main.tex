\section*{Status}

The intended finite-support form of Problem 3 in Veeorg's
\emph{Characterizations of Daugavet- and delta-points in Lipschitz-free
spaces} is answered affirmatively by Abrahamsen, Aliaga, Lima, Martiny,
Perreau, Prochazka, and Veeorg, \emph{Delta-points and their implications for
the geometry of Banach spaces}, arXiv:2303.00511, Theorem 6.9.

\section*{Source question}

For a pointed metric space \(M\), write \(\F(M)\) for its Lipschitz-free
space and
\[
 m_{xy}=\frac{\delta_x-\delta_y}{d(x,y)}
\]
for a molecule.  A unit vector is a \(\Delta\)-point if every slice of the
unit ball containing it contains vectors arbitrarily close to distance two
from it.

Immediately after an example of a finite convex combination that is a
\(\Delta\)-point despite its displayed summands not being \(\Delta\)-points,
the source asks on page 20:

\begin{sourceproblem}
Can the point instead be represented as a finite convex combination
\[
  \mu=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i m_{x_i y_i},
  \qquad \lambda_i>0,\quad \sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i=1,
\]
in which every molecule \(m_{x_i y_i}\) is a \(\Delta\)-point?
\end{sourceproblem}

The displayed source formula literally restricts \(\mu\) to \(\M(M)\), the
set of individual molecules.  Read literally, that would be tautological.
The surrounding paragraph concerns finitely supported molecular
combinations, and the later answering paper, coauthored by Veeorg, explicitly
identifies source Problem 3 as the question for finitely supported
\(\Delta\)-points.  That later-author-confirmed formulation is the scope
recorded here.

\section*{Exact literature answer}

On page 34 of arXiv:2303.00511, the authors state:

\begin{answertheorem}
Suppose that \(\mu\in S_{\F(M)}\) is a \(\Delta\)-point with finite support.
Then \(\mu\) can be written as a finite convex combination of
\(\Delta\)-molecules in \(S_{\F(M)}\).
\end{answertheorem}

The paper explicitly labels this theorem as corresponding to Problem 3 of the
source and says that it answers the question positively.  Thus the intended
finite-support question is completely settled.

\section*{Proof mechanism in the answering paper}

The answering paper introduces auxiliary metrics \(b_\alpha\),
\(0<\alpha<1\), satisfying
\[
 (1-\alpha)d(x,y)\leq b_\alpha(x,y)\leq d(x,y).
\]
Its preceding characterization implies that a finitely supported
\(\Delta\)-point \(\mu\) has
\[
 \|\mu\|_{\F(M,b_\alpha)}=1-\alpha.
\]
Because \(\mu\) has finite support, the normalized vector
\(\mu/(1-\alpha)\) admits a finite convex decomposition into
\(b_\alpha\)-molecules.  Rewriting that decomposition with the original
metric and comparing norms gives
\[
 1=\|\mu\|_{\F(M,d)}
 \leq \sum_i\lambda_i
       \frac{(1-\alpha)d(x_i,y_i)}{b_\alpha(x_i,y_i)}
 \leq \sum_i\lambda_i=1.
\]
Every inequality is therefore an equality, so
\[
 b_\alpha(x_i,y_i)=(1-\alpha)d(x_i,y_i)
\]
for each edge in the decomposition.  The paper's metric characterization of
\(\Delta\)-molecules says precisely that this equality forces each
\(m_{x_i y_i}\) to be a \(\Delta\)-point.

\section*{Scope not answered}

Problem 2 of the source asks whether every slice containing an arbitrary
\(\Delta\)-point contains molecules of arbitrarily small metric length.
Theorem 6.9 assumes finite support and does not answer this all-elements
problem.  The answering paper emphasizes that not every unit-sphere element
of a Lipschitz-free space is even a convex sum of molecules.
