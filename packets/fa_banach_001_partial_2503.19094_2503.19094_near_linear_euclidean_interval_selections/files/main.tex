\section{Source problem}

The source is P.~Shvartsman, \emph{Efficient Algorithms for Lipschitz
Selections of Set-Valued Mappings in $\R^2$}, arXiv:2503.19094
\cite{Shvartsman}. Its Theorem 1.3 gives a quadratic-work, quadratic-storage
gap algorithm for half-plane-valued maps. On physical PDF page 4 (source
TeX line 1341), Remark 1.4 asks:

\begin{quote}
``Can we replace the Projection Algorithm with an algorithm requiring at most
$O(N\log N)$ operations and $O(N)$ storage, similar to the Approximate
Selection Algorithm described in [35]?''
\end{quote}

The domain in the example is an $N$-point subset of $\R^n$ with Euclidean
distance. The remark then says explicitly that the answer is unclear even for
$D=1$, when every value is a closed interval in $\R$.

\section{Definitions and output convention}

Let $(\M,\rho)$ be a finite metric space. A weighted connected graph
$G=(\M,\E)$ is an \emph{upper $t$-spanner} for $\rho$ if its shortest-path
metric $d_G$ satisfies
\[
  \rho(x,y)\le d_G(x,y)\le t\rho(x,y)\qquad(x,y\in\M).
\]
A usual metric spanner, whose edge $xy$ has weight $\rho(x,y)$, has the first
inequality automatically by the triangle inequality.

An interval is written $I_x=[a_x,b_x]$, allowing
$a_x=-\infty$ and $b_x=+\infty$. A selection is a map $f$ with
$f(x)\in I_x$. Given $\lambda\ge0$, the desired gap output is either

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item a sound certificate that no $\lambda$-Lipschitz selection exists; or
\item a selection with Lipschitz constant at most $t\lambda$.
\end{enumerate}

This is the same one-sided output convention as the source: the algorithm is
allowed to return a controlled-factor success even when no selection exists
at the smaller constant $\lambda$.

\section{Proof intuition}

For scalar intervals, all global compatibility information collapses to one
min-plus distance transform. With the complete metric it is
\[
  x\longmapsto \min_y\{b_y+\lambda\rho(x,y)\}.
\]
Replacing $\rho$ by an upper spanner metric can only increase this transform.
Consequently, failure for the spanner is still a genuine failure for the
original metric. On the other hand, success is Lipschitz for the spanner
metric and loses only its stretch factor when returned to $\rho$.

The transform is exactly a multi-source shortest-path computation. A
fixed-dimensional Euclidean $(1+\varepsilon)$-spanner has only linearly many
edges and is constructible in near-linear time, so one shortest-path pass
delivers the requested complexity. The remaining $\R^2$ half-plane problem
does not collapse to a single scalar transform because its constraints have
varying normal directions.

\section{Spanner reduction}

\begin{lemma}[exact interval criterion]\label{lem:criterion}
Suppose all $I_x=[a_x,b_x]$ are nonempty. There is a
$\lambda$-Lipschitz selection for $(\M,\rho)$ if and only if
\[
 a_x\le b_y+\lambda\rho(x,y)\qquad(x,y\in\M).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Necessity follows from
$a_x\le f(x)\le f(y)+\lambda\rho(x,y)\le
b_y+\lambda\rho(x,y)$.

If at least one $b_y$ is finite, put
\[
 u(x)=\min_{y:\,b_y<+\infty}\{b_y+\lambda\rho(x,y)\}.
\]
The assumed inequalities give $a_x\le u(x)$, while the term $y=x$ gives
$u(x)\le b_x$ whenever $b_x$ is finite. Thus $u(x)\in I_x$. The triangle
inequality gives $u(x)\le u(z)+\lambda\rho(x,z)$ and its symmetric version,
so $u$ is $\lambda$-Lipschitz. If every $b_y=+\infty$, the constant equal to
the largest finite lower endpoint is a selection; use zero if all lower
endpoints are $-\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[sparse-spanner interval algorithm]\label{thm:graph}
Let $G=(\M,\E)$ be an upper $t$-spanner for a metric $\rho$, let
$I_x=[a_x,b_x]$ be nonempty closed intervals, and let $\lambda\ge0$. There is
an algorithm with work
\[
 O\bigl((|\M|+|\E|)\log |\M|\bigr)
\]
and storage $O(|\M|+|\E|)$ which produces one of the following:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item a correct conclusion that no $\lambda$-Lipschitz selection exists for
$(\M,\rho)$; or
\item a selection $f$ with $\|f\|_{\Lip(\M,\rho)}\le t\lambda$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If every upper endpoint is infinite, use the constant selection from
Lemma~\ref{lem:criterion}. Otherwise define
\[
 u_G(x)=\min_{y:\,b_y<+\infty}\{b_y+\lambda d_G(x,y)\}.
\]
If $a_x>u_G(x)$ for some $x$, choose a minimizer $y$. Since
$d_G(x,y)\ge\rho(x,y)$,
\[
 a_x>b_y+\lambda d_G(x,y)
       \ge b_y+\lambda\rho(x,y).
\]
This violates the necessary condition in Lemma~\ref{lem:criterion}, so the
no-go conclusion is sound.

Otherwise $u_G(x)\in I_x$: the lower inequality is the passed test, and the
upper inequality follows from the term $y=x$ whenever $b_x$ is finite. As in
the proof of the lemma,
\[
 |u_G(x)-u_G(z)|\le\lambda d_G(x,z)
                    \le t\lambda\rho(x,z).
\]
Thus $u_G$ is the promised selection.

For the complexity claim, let $B$ be the smallest finite upper endpoint.
Initialize each vertex $y$ with finite upper endpoint at label $b_y-B\ge0$
and give every graph edge $e$ length $\lambda w(e)\ge0$. A single
multi-source version of Dijkstra's label-setting algorithm \cite{Dijkstra}
computes $u_G-B$. Its work and storage have the stated bounds.
\end{proof}

\section{Near-linear Euclidean consequence}

\begin{corollary}[the source's interval case]\label{cor:euclidean}
Fix $n\in\mathbb N$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Given an $N$-point set
$\M\subset\R^n$, nonempty closed intervals $I_x$, and $\lambda\ge0$, one can
produce either

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item a sound certificate that no $\lambda$-Lipschitz interval selection
exists; or
\item an interval selection with Euclidean Lipschitz constant at most
$(1+\varepsilon)\lambda$,
\end{enumerate}

using $O_{n,\varepsilon}(N\log N)$ operations and $O_{n,\varepsilon}(N)$
storage.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Standard fixed-dimensional Euclidean spanner constructions produce a
$(1+\varepsilon)$-spanner with $O_{n,\varepsilon}(N)$ edges in
$O_{n,\varepsilon}(N\log N)$ time and linear storage
\cite{AryaEtAl}. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:graph} with
$t=1+\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

This gives a full affirmative answer to the $D=1$ case that Remark 1.4
explicitly singles out. When the domain lies in $\R$, sorting the points and
joining consecutive points produces an exact $1$-spanner, so the success
constant improves to $\lambda$ itself.

The same proof coordinate by coordinate gives another extension: for fixed
$D$, if every value is an axis-aligned box in $(\R^D,\|\cdot\|_\infty)$, the
same gap algorithm uses $O_{n,D,\varepsilon}(N\log N)$ work and linear storage
and returns a $(1+\varepsilon)\lambda$-selection. A no-go in one coordinate is
a valid no-go for the box problem.

\section{Verification and upgrade attempts}

The script
\texttt{code/verify\_spanner\_interval\_selection.py} checks 600 random line
instances using the exact path spanner and 600 random planar instances using
small greedy $1.8$-spanners. It includes rays and the whole line. In every
case it verifies the spanner inequalities, compares no-go outputs with the
complete-metric criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:criterion}, and checks interval
membership and the promised Lipschitz bound. It prints
\texttt{VERDICT: PASS}. The experiment is a regression check, not the proof.

Eight focused upgrade routes are recorded in the run's \texttt{attempts/}
directory. The first four establish the exact scalar criterion, the abstract
spanner transfer, the Euclidean near-linear bound, and the unbounded/box
extensions. The remaining routes test an exact factor-one distance transform,
a sparse half-plane feasibility reduction, WSPD compression of the source
algorithm, and approximate convex optimization. They do not yield the full
arbitrary-half-plane algorithm.

\section{Literature and novelty check}

The bounded search covered the exact title and arXiv id, the exact phrase
``remains unclear even in the seemingly simpler one dimensional setting'',
the author together with interval/Lipschitz-selection/near-linear keywords,
and combinations with Euclidean spanners and shortest paths. Searches used
arXiv and primary journal or conference pages. No later paper stating this
interval subproblem or the spanner solution was found.

Novelty confidence is moderate-to-high. The two ingredients are elementary
once juxtaposed, and the Euclidean spanner theorem is classical, so an
unindexed prior observation remains possible. However, the 2025 source itself
explicitly labels the interval case unclear, and the bounded 2026 search found
no subsequent answer.

\section{Limitations and human-review recommendation}

The near-linear constants assume fixed ambient dimension $n$ and fixed
$\varepsilon$; the claim is not a dimension-uniform algorithm. The result
fully resolves only interval-valued maps and, by products, axis-aligned boxes.
It does not solve the source's arbitrary half-plane or polygon problem in
$\R^2$.

For that remaining case, spanner edges reduce the problem to a sparse linear
feasibility system, but no exact near-linear solver or compressed version of
the Projection Algorithm is supplied. Human review should check the direction
of the two spanner inequalities in the no-go and success arguments and confirm
that the cited Euclidean spanner construction matches the source's fixed-
dimensional real-RAM model. All other steps are elementary.
