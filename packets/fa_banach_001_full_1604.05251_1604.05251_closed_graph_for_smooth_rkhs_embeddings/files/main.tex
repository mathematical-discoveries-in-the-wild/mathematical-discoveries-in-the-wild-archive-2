\section*{Source Problem}

The source is Carl-Johann Simon-Gabriel and Bernhard Sch\"olkopf,
\emph{Kernel Distribution Embeddings: Universal Kernels, Characteristic
Kernels and Kernel Metrics on Distributions}, arXiv:1604.05251
\cite{SimonGabrielScholkopf}.  After Proposition~4, the authors ask whether,
on page 9, mere set inclusion of the RKHS in a smooth function space forces
continuous inclusion.  Their exact question is:

\begin{quote}
``We do not know whether, in general, $\mathcal H_k\subset C^m_*$ implies
$\mathcal H_k\hookrightarrow C^m_*$.''
\end{quote}

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/open_problem_crop.png}
\end{center}

In their notation, the symbol $C^m_*$ means either $C^m(\Omega)$, with
uniform convergence on compact sets of all derivatives through order $m$,
or $C^m_0(\Omega)$, with uniform convergence on all of $\Omega$ and all
those derivatives vanishing at infinity.  Here $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$
is open, and $m\in\mathbb N_0\cup\{\infty\}$.

\section*{Result Type}

This packet gives a \textbf{full affirmative answer}.  No smoothness or even
continuity assumption on the kernel is required beyond the stated set
inclusion.

\section*{Proof Intuition}

The only possible obstruction is discontinuity of the set-theoretic identity
map from the RKHS norm to the smooth topology.  But such discontinuity cannot
hide in a sequence with two different limits.  RKHS-norm convergence always
determines the pointwise limit, because point evaluation is bounded; smooth
target convergence also determines the pointwise limit, because its
zeroth-order seminorms are part of the topology.  Hence the identity map has
closed graph.  The source spaces and both target alternatives are Fr\'echet,
so the closed graph theorem supplies continuity automatically.  The only
technical check is completeness of $C^m_0$ when $m=\infty$.

\section*{Main Theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $K:\Omega\times\Omega\to\C$ be a positive-definite kernel with RKHS
\(\Hk\), and let $m\in\mathbb N_0\cup\{\infty\}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If \(\Hk\subset C^m(\Omega)\), then the canonical inclusion
\[
J:\Hk\longrightarrow C^m(\Omega),\qquad Jf=f,
\]
is continuous.
\item If \(\Hk\subset C^m_0(\Omega)\), then the canonical inclusion
\(J:\Hk\to C^m_0(\Omega)\) is continuous.
\end{enumerate}
Thus \(\Hk\subset C^m_*(\Omega)\) implies
\(\Hk\hookrightarrow C^m_*(\Omega)\) for both meanings of the star in the
source question.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $E=C^m(\Omega)$ in the first case and $E=C^m_0(\Omega)$ in the
second.  The RKHS \(\Hk\) is a Hilbert space, hence a Fr\'echet space.  The
space $E$, with precisely the topology used in the source paper, is also a
Fr\'echet space.  For completeness, this standard fact is checked below.

We show that the graph of the linear map $J:\Hk\to E$ is closed.  Suppose
that a sequence \((f_n)\subset\Hk\) satisfies
\[
 f_n\longrightarrow f\quad\hbox{in }\Hk,
 \qquad
 Jf_n\longrightarrow g\quad\hbox{in }E.
\]
For every $x\in\Omega$, the reproducing property and Cauchy--Schwarz give
\[
 |f_n(x)-f(x)|
 =\bigl|\langle f_n-f,K(\cdot,x)\rangle_K\bigr|
 \leq \sqrt{K(x,x)}\,\norm{f_n-f}_K\longrightarrow0.
\]
On the other hand, convergence in either target $E$ implies pointwise
convergence of the zeroth-order functions, so $f_n(x)\to g(x)$.  Therefore
$f(x)=g(x)$ for every $x$, or $Jf=g$.  The graph is closed.

The closed graph theorem for Fr\'echet spaces
\cite[Chapter~2]{Rudin} now says that $J$ is continuous.
\end{proof}

\section*{Why the Target Spaces Are Fr\'echet}

For $C^m(\Omega)$, choose a compact exhaustion \((L_j)_{j\geq1}\) of the
open set \(\Omega\).  Its topology is generated by the countable seminorms
\[
 p_{j,r}(f)=\max_{|\alpha|\leq r}\sup_{x\in L_j}|D^\alpha f(x)|,
 \qquad j\geq1,\quad r\leq m.
\]
For finite $m$ only finitely many derivative orders occur, while for
$m=\infty$ the family remains countable.  A Cauchy sequence has locally
uniform limits for every derivative; the elementary local uniform
differentiation theorem shows that these limits are the derivatives of its
zeroth-order limit.  Hence $C^m(\Omega)$ is complete and metrizable.

For $C^m_0(\Omega)$, use
\[
 q_r(f)=\max_{|\alpha|\leq r}\norm{D^\alpha f}_\infty,
 \qquad r\leq m.
\]
When $m<\infty$, $q_m$ is a complete norm; when $m=\infty$, the
countable family \((q_r)\) gives a complete metrizable locally convex
topology.  Indeed, a Cauchy sequence has uniform limits for all derivatives,
the same differentiation argument identifies them correctly, and a uniform
limit of functions vanishing at infinity again vanishes at infinity.  Thus
this target is Fr\'echet as well.

\section*{Quantitative Consequences}

The theorem says more concretely that every target seminorm is controlled by
the RKHS norm.  In the $C^m(\Omega)$ case, for every compact
$L\subset\Omega$ and finite $r\leq m$, there is a constant $C_{L,r}$
such that
\[
 \max_{|\alpha|\leq r}\sup_{x\in L}|D^\alpha f(x)|
 \leq C_{L,r}\norm f_K
 \qquad(f\in\Hk).
\]
In the $C^m_0(\Omega)$ case, there is for each finite $r\leq m$ a global
constant $C_r$ such that
\[
 \max_{|\alpha|\leq r}\norm{D^\alpha f}_\infty
 \leq C_r\norm f_K.
\]
In particular, every derivative evaluation $f\mapsto D^\alpha f(x)$ is a
bounded functional on \(\Hk\) and therefore has a Riesz representer in the
RKHS.

\section*{Audit of the Argument}

\begin{itemize}
\item RKHS-norm convergence gives pointwise convergence for every kernel,
because each point evaluation is bounded by definition.  Kernel continuity
is not being smuggled into the proof.
\item Only zeroth-order pointwise convergence is needed to identify the two
limits.  The hypothesis that all RKHS elements lie in the target makes the
canonical map everywhere defined.
\item Both values $m<\infty$ and $m=\infty$ are covered.  In the latter
case the seminorm families are countable because multi-indices in
\(\mathbb N_0^d\) are countable.
\item Vanishing at infinity is stable under uniform limits, so the
\(C^m_0\) target is complete rather than merely a subspace of a complete
product.
\item The conclusion is exactly continuity of the canonical inclusion.  It
does not assert joint \((m,m)\)-smoothness of $K$, which would require a
separate argument.
\end{itemize}
