\section{Questions and outcomes}

\textbf{Source.} B. Lemmens and R. Nussbaum,
\emph{Continuity of the cone spectral radius}, arXiv:1107.4532~\cite{LN}.
For a homogeneous order-preserving map $f:C\to C$, its cone spectrum is
\[
 \sigma_C(f)=\{\lambda\geq0:f(x)=\lambda x\text{ for some }0\ne x\in C\}.
\]
The source asks (i) whether the Bonsall radius $\widetilde r_C(f)$ always
equals the supremal orbital growth radius $r_C(f)$ for general nonlinear
maps, and (ii) which finite-dimensional closed cones admit a map whose cone
spectrum contains a continuum.

\textbf{Literature answer to (i).} Gripenberg's Example~3.1 is a continuous,
order-preserving, degree-one homogeneous map on a closed pointed convex cone
in a Banach space.  His Lemma~3.5 proves
\[
 \widetilde r_C(f)\geq1\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_C(f)\leq\frac12,
\]
so the answer is negative~\cite{Gripenberg}.  This directly resolves the
open question on p.~2 of the source.

For (ii), the source proves that every polyhedral cone has finite cone
spectrum for every order-preserving homogeneous map, and constructs an
infinite spectrum on every nonpolyhedral cone.  It does not decide when the
spectrum can contain a nondegenerate compact connected set (equivalently, an
interval, since the spectrum lies in $\R$).  We prove a complete answer for
symmetric cones and show that nonpolyhedrality alone is insufficient.

\section{The symmetric-cone classification}

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional Euclidean Jordan algebra with trace inner
product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, unit $e$, and cone of squares
$\K=\{x^2:x\in V\}$.  This is a symmetric cone.  Powers of elements of $\K$
are defined by spectral calculus; see~\cite{Gowda,FK}.

\begin{lemma}[L\"owner--Heinz at exponent $1/2$]\label{lem:sqrt}
If $0\leq x\leq y$ in $\K$, then $x^{1/2}\leq y^{1/2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First suppose $x,y$ are interior.  Inversion reverses the symmetric-cone
order.  Indeed, with $P$ the quadratic representation, $x\leq y$ gives
$e\leq z=P(x^{-1/2})y$.  Spectral calculus gives $z^{-1}\leq e$; applying
the positive map $P(x^{-1/2})$ and using
$(P(x^{-1/2})y)^{-1}=P(x^{1/2})y^{-1}$ yields $y^{-1}\leq x^{-1}$.

For scalars $s>0$,
\[
 \sqrt{s}=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\frac{s}{s+t}\,dt.
\]
The same identity holds by Jordan spectral calculus.  Since
\[
 x\circ(x+te)^{-1}=e-t(x+te)^{-1},
\]
inverse order reversal shows that the integrand is order-preserving in $x$.
Integration proves the claim in the interior, and replacing $x,y$ by
$x+\varepsilon e,y+\varepsilon e$ and letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$
proves the boundary case.
\end{proof}

Fix $a$ in the interior of $\K$, put $\ell_a(x)=\langle a,x\rangle$, and
define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:map}
 F_a(x)=\bigl(\ell_a(x)x\bigr)^{1/2}\qquad(x\in\K).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Exact spectrum of the square-root map]\label{lem:spectrum}
$F_a$ is continuous, order-preserving, and homogeneous of degree one, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-spectrum}
 \sigma_{\K}(F_a)
 =\{\sqrt{\langle a,c\rangle}:c\ne0\text{ is a Jordan idempotent}\}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Continuity is spectral calculus, and $F_a(tx)=tF_a(x)$ for $t\geq0$.  The
cone is self-dual, so $x\leq y$ implies $\ell_a(x)\leq\ell_a(y)$.  Moreover,
\[
 \ell_a(y)y-\ell_a(x)x
 =\ell_a(y)(y-x)+(\ell_a(y)-\ell_a(x))x\in\K.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:sqrt} therefore proves order preservation.

Write $x=\sum_i\mu_i c_i$, with $\mu_i\geq0$ and $(c_i)$ a Jordan frame.
If $F_a(x)=\lambda x$ and $x\ne0$, then $\lambda>0$ because $a$ is interior.
Squaring gives
\[
 \ell_a(x)\mu_i=\lambda^2\mu_i^2\quad\text{for every }i.
\]
All positive $\mu_i$ are consequently equal, so $x=q c$ for $q>0$ and a
nonzero idempotent $c$.  Direct substitution gives
$F_a(qc)=\sqrt{\langle a,c\rangle}\,qc$.  This proves both inclusions in
\eqref{eq:exact-spectrum}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Complete answer for symmetric cones]\label{thm:symmetric}
For a finite-dimensional symmetric cone $\K$, the following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\K$ is nonpolyhedral.
\item There is a continuous order-preserving homogeneous map $F:\K\to\K$
whose cone spectrum contains a nondegenerate interval.
\end{enumerate}
When (1) holds, $F$ may be chosen in the explicit form~\eqref{eq:map}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $\K$ is polyhedral, Theorem~5.5 of the source says that every such spectrum
is finite, so (2) is impossible.

Conversely, decompose $V$ into simple Jordan factors.  If every factor has
rank one, $\K$ is an orthant and hence polyhedral.  Thus a nonpolyhedral
$\K$ has a simple factor of rank at least two.  Choose a Jordan frame
$c_1,c_2,\ldots$ in that factor.  The Peirce space $V_{12}$ is nonzero; choose
$z\in V_{12}$ normalized by $z^2=c_1+c_2$.  A direct use of the Peirce
multiplication rules shows that
\[
 c(\theta)=\cos^2\!\theta\,c_1+\sin^2\!\theta\,c_2
 +\sin\theta\cos\theta\,z,
 \qquad0\leq\theta\leq\frac\pi2,
\]
satisfies $c(\theta)^2=c(\theta)$ and has trace one, so it is primitive.

Choose $a$ interior whose coefficients at $c_1,c_2$ are
$0<\alpha<\beta$ and whose other Jordan-frame coefficients are positive.
Peirce orthogonality gives
\[
 \langle a,c(\theta)\rangle
 =\alpha\cos^2\!\theta+\beta\sin^2\!\theta.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:spectrum} now shows that
$[\sqrt\alpha,\sqrt\beta]\subseteq\sigma_{\K}(F_a)$.
\end{proof}

This includes Lorentz cones, real/complex/quaternionic positive-semidefinite
matrix cones, and the exceptional symmetric cone.  It generalizes the
$2\times2$ real positive-semidefinite example in the source from one cone to
the entire symmetric-cone class.

\section{Nonpolyhedrality is not sufficient in general}

The following obstruction explains why Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} does not
settle every finite-dimensional cone.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:counter}
There is a closed pointed full-dimensional nonpolyhedral cone $C\subset\R^3$
such that $\sigma_C(f)$ is countable for every order-preserving homogeneous
map $f:C\to C$, even without assuming continuity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the unit circle of $\R^2$, let
\[
 S=\{(\cos(1/n),\sin(1/n)):n\geq1\}
 \cup\{(1,0),(-1,0),(0,-1)\},
 \qquad B=\operatorname{conv}(S).
\]
The set $S$ is compact.  Every point of $S$ is exposed in $B$: the tangent
line to the unit disk at that point meets the disk only there.  Conversely,
Milman's converse to Krein--Milman gives $\operatorname{ext}(B)\subseteq S$.
Thus $B$ has countably infinitely many extreme points.  A proper face of a
planar compact convex set is either an extreme singleton or a line-segment
edge determined by two extreme endpoints, so $B$ has only countably many
faces.

Take the cone over this base,
\[
 C=\{t(u,1):t\geq0, u\in B\}\subset\R^3.
\]
It is closed, pointed, and full-dimensional.  It is nonpolyhedral because it
has infinitely many extreme rays, while its faces---and hence its
parts---are countable.  Lemma~4.3 and Corollary~5.4 of the source show that
two eigenvectors in the same part must have the same eigenvalue.  Each part
therefore contributes at most one point to $\sigma_C(f)$, proving
countability.
\end{proof}

Thus ``nonpolyhedral'' cannot be the answer to the source's general problem.
The unresolved classification must distinguish the much finer face/extension
geometry of arbitrary cones.

\section{Verification, novelty, and scope}

The order-preserving calculation for $F_a$ is explicit.  Its exact spectrum
follows coefficient-by-coefficient from Jordan spectral decomposition.  The
Peirce path is checked by $c_i\circ z=z/2$, $c_1\circ c_2=0$, and
$z^2=c_1+c_2$.  Proposition~\ref{prop:counter} uses only elementary planar
convexity plus the source's same-part eigenvalue lemma.

A bounded search through 11 August 2026 found Gripenberg's literature answer,
the source's matrix example, its later book presentation, and papers on the
different complementarity notion of cone spectrum.  No source found states
Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}, the exact formula~\eqref{eq:exact-spectrum}, or
Proposition~\ref{prop:counter}.  This is a bounded novelty check, not an
exhaustive claim.

\textbf{Scope.} Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} is a full classification only for
symmetric cones.  Proposition~\ref{prop:counter} proves that the general
finite-dimensional question requires more than polyhedral versus
nonpolyhedral.  No full classification of arbitrary cones is claimed.
