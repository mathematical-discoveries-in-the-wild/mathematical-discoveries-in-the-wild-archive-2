\paragraph{Status.}
\textbf{Full negative answer to the precise semi-skew nonexistence question.}
For every two-dimensional semi-skew operator with unequal weights, both
coordinate axes solve the paper's fixed-point equation.  In fact, these are
the only nonempty solutions.  They are unbounded and contain the origin on
their boundary, exactly outside the class covered by the paper's
nonexistence theorem.

\section*{Source question}
For a nonempty \(S\subseteq X\), Reem and Reich [1] use the polar
\[
 S\polar=\{z\in X:\langle z,s\rangle\le1\ \hbox{for every }s\in S\}
\]
and study
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source}
 C=(GC)\polar.
\end{equation}
Their Proposition 8.5 excludes solutions that are bounded and contain
\(0\) in their interior when \(G\) is semi-skew.  Remark 10.2 asks whether
one can prove or disprove that a semi-skew \(G\) has no nonempty solution at
all.

\section*{Proof intuition}
The counterexample itself is one line: a semi-skew matrix swaps the two
coordinate axes, and the polar of a full line is its orthogonal line.  Thus
each axis returns to itself after applying \(G\) and then polarizing.

There is also a rigid classification behind this observation.  Any solution
of \eqref{eq:source} is automatically closed, convex, and contains zero.
Bipolarity and the linear transformation rule for polars show that it must
be invariant under \(G^{-1}G^T\).  For unequal semi-skew weights this is a
hyperbolic diagonal map, expanding one coordinate while contracting the
other and reversing both signs.  Iteration, rescaling toward zero, and
closedness force both coordinate projections of every point back into the
set.  Convexity then leaves only the four coordinate subspaces; direct
substitution retains exactly the two axes.

\begin{theorem}[exact semi-skew classification in \(\R^2\)]\label{thm:main}
Let \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\in\R\) have the same sign and satisfy
\(\alpha_1\ne\alpha_2\).  Define
\[
 G=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha_2\\-\alpha_1&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 G(x_1,x_2)=(\alpha_2x_2,-\alpha_1x_1).
\]
This is the two-dimensional semi-skew operator of Definition 8.1 in [1].
Among all nonempty subsets \(C\subseteq\R^2\), the solutions of
\(C=(GC)\polar\) are exactly
\[
 L_1=\R\times\{0\}
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 L_2=\{0\}\times\R.
\]
In particular, the nonexistence statement proposed for consideration in
Remark 10.2 is false.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First, both displayed lines are solutions.  Indeed,
\[
 GL_1=L_2,\qquad GL_2=L_1.
\]
For every linear subspace \(L\subseteq\R^2\), its polar in the source's sense
is \(L^\perp\): if \(z\) is not orthogonal to some \(\ell\in L\), scaling
\(\ell\) through both signs violates \(\langle z,\ell\rangle\le1\), while
orthogonal vectors give inner product zero.  Hence
\[
 (GL_1)\polar=L_2^\perp=L_1,\qquad
 (GL_2)\polar=L_1^\perp=L_2.
\]
Taking, for example, \((\alpha_1,\alpha_2)=(1,2)\) already disproves the
proposed universal nonexistence.

We prove that there are no other solutions.  Suppose that a nonempty
\(C\subseteq\R^2\) satisfies \eqref{eq:source}.  As a polar, \(C\) is closed
and convex and contains zero.  Therefore \(GC\) has the same three
properties, and the bipolar theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bipolar}
 C\polar=((GC)\polar)\polar=GC.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, the elementary transformation rule
\((GC)\polar=(G^T)^{-1}C\polar\), combined with
\eqref{eq:source}, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transpose}
 C\polar=G^TC.
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:bipolar}--\eqref{eq:transpose} imply
\[
 C=HC,\qquad
 H:=G^{-1}G^T
   =\begin{pmatrix}
      -r&0\\0&-r^{-1}
     \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad r:=\frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha_1}>0,\quad r\ne1.
\]
Replacing \(H\) by \(H^{-1}\), which also leaves \(C\) invariant, allows us
to assume \(r>1\).

Fix \(x=(x_1,x_2)\in C\).  Since \(H^{2n}x\in C\), convexity and \(0\in C\)
give
\[
 r^{-2n}H^{2n}x=(x_1,r^{-4n}x_2)\in C.
\]
Closedness and \(n\to\infty\) yield \((x_1,0)\in C\).  Applying the same
argument to \(H^{-2n}\) yields \((0,x_2)\in C\).

If \(C\) contains a nonzero point of \(L_1\), invariance under all integer
powers of \(H\), followed by contraction toward zero inside the convex set,
shows that it contains every positive and negative multiple of that point;
hence \(L_1\subseteq C\).  The same statement holds for \(L_2\).  Thus,
according to which coordinate projections occur, \(C\) is one of
\[
 \{0\},\quad L_1,\quad L_2,\quad\R^2.
\]
(If both projections occur, convexity combines the two full axes to give
all of \(\R^2\).)  Finally, \(\{0\}\) and \(\R^2\) are interchanged by
polarity and so do not solve \eqref{eq:source}, whereas \(L_1,L_2\) do.
\end{proof}

\section*{Verification and literature status}
The bundled exact SymPy script checks the formula for \(G^{-1}G^T\), the
axis swaps, the polar-subspace calculations, the failure of the zero and
full subspaces, and representative positive and negative unequal weights.
These checks are independent sanity checks; the proof above is symbolic.

The result answers only the precise semi-skew subquestion of Remark 10.2.
The paper's much broader request to classify solutions for arbitrary
non-positive-definite \(G\) remains open.  Exact-title, semi-skew,
coordinate-axis, DOI, and citation searches through 13 August 2026 found no
later source giving this answer or the two-dimensional classification.  The
search was bounded rather than a systematic bibliographic review.
