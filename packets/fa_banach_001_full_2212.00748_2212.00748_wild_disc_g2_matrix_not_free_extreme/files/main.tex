\title{An exact non-free matrix extreme point of the two-variable wild disc}
\author{Solution packet for arXiv:2212.00748}
\date{13 August 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Epperly, Evert, Helton, and Klep ask whether a real free spectrahedron in two
variables can have a matrix extreme point which is not free extreme, and they
``strongly strongly conjecture'' this for the wild disc.  We give such a
point at level $n=8$.  The entries are specified exactly: one coordinate is
rational, while the other is a symmetric zero of a quadratic matrix equation
in a certified rational ball.  Exact rational residual bounds prove
existence of that zero and prove full column rank of the matrix-extreme
system.  A strict $15<16$ equation count then proves that the point is not
Arveson extreme and hence not free extreme.  All proof-critical inequalities
are checked with integer and rational arithmetic by the accompanying script.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question and the answer}

Let
\[
A_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&0&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The associated wild disc is
\[
 \D_A(n)=\left\{(X,Y)\in\Sym_n(\R)^2:
 L_A(X,Y)=\begin{pmatrix}I&X&Y\\X&I&0\\Y&0&I\end{pmatrix}\succeq0\right\}.
\]
Equivalently, by a Schur complement,
\[
 (X,Y)\in\D_A(n)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I-X^2-Y^2\succeq0.
\]
This free spectrahedron is bounded.  Section 5.3 of the authors' current
manuscript says that the existence of non-free matrix extreme points for
$g=2$ is open and makes the specific wild-disc conjecture; see the included
source PDF and the page-17 source image.

\begin{theorem}[Two-variable wild-disc conjecture]
There exist real symmetric $8\times8$ matrices $X,Y$ such that $(X,Y)$ is a
matrix extreme point of the wild disc but is not a free extreme point.
\end{theorem}

The rest of the packet gives the exact construction and proof.

\section{A certified exact square root}

On $\Sym_8(\R)$ use the entrywise norm
$\normmax{H}=\max_{i,j}|H_{ij}|$.  Let
\[
 \mathcal L_Q(H)=QH+HQ,\qquad E=Q^2-P.
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:root}
The operator $\mathcal L_Q$ is invertible.  There is a symmetric $H$ with
\[
 \normmax{H}<3.014\cdot10^{-12},\qquad (Q+H)^2=P.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The exact checker represents $\mathcal L_Q$ on the 36 upper-triangular
coordinates.  From a rational approximate inverse $T$ it verifies
\[
 \lVert I-T\mathcal L_Q\rVert_\infty<1.827\cdot10^{-9},
 \qquad \lVert\mathcal L_Q^{-1}\rVert_\infty<1192.
\]
This is a standard Neumann-series certificate and in particular proves
invertibility.  The checker also obtains
\[
 \rho:=\normmax{E}<1.265\cdot10^{-15}.
\]
Let $\beta$ denote the exact rational inverse bound produced by this
certificate (so $\beta<1192$), and let $r=2\beta\rho$.  On the closed
radius-$r$ ball in $\Sym_8(\R)$ consider
\[
 \Phi(H)=-\mathcal L_Q^{-1}(E+H^2).
\]
For matrices in that ball,
\[
 \normmax{H^2-G^2}\le 16r\normmax{H-G}.
\]
The exact certificate checks
\[
 q:=4\cdot8\,\lVert\mathcal L_Q^{-1}\rVert_\infty^2\rho
 <5.748\cdot10^{-8}<1,
 \qquad r<3.014\cdot10^{-12}.
\]
Consequently $\Phi$ maps the ball to itself and has Lipschitz constant at
most $q$.  Banach's fixed-point theorem supplies $H=\Phi(H)$, which is
equivalent to $(Q+H)^2=P$.
\end{proof}

Set $Y=Q+H$.  We now have the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:defect}
 I-X^2-Y^2=Z^TZ\succeq0.
\end{equation}
Thus $(X,Y)\in\D_A(8)$.

\section{The matrix-extreme certificate}

The columns of $K=K_0/42$, where
\[
K_0=\begin{pmatrix}
39&34&58&31&62\\
-108&-92&-236&10&-190\\
-129&-64&-208&17&-176\\
42&0&0&0&0\\
0&42&0&0&0\\
0&0&42&0&0\\
0&0&0&42&0\\
0&0&0&0&42
\end{pmatrix},
\]
form a basis of $\ker Z$.  Indeed $ZK=0$, and a $3\times3$ minor of the
integer numerator $R$ has determinant $-42$, so $\operatorname{rank}Z=3$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:defect} therefore gives
\[
 \dim\ker L_A(X,Y)=\dim\ker(Z^TZ)=5,
\]
with kernel basis
\[
 \mathcal K(Y)=\begin{pmatrix}K\\-XK\\-YK\end{pmatrix}.
\]

We use Theorem 2.6(3) of the source paper.  It says that $(X,Y)$ is matrix
extreme exactly when the only symmetric triple $(B_0,B_1,B_2)$ satisfying
\begin{align}
 B_0v-B_1Xv-B_2Yv&=0,\label{eq:m1}\\
 B_1v-B_0Xv&=0,\label{eq:m2}\\
 B_2v-B_0Yv&=0\label{eq:m3}
\end{align}
for every $v\in\ker Z$, together with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace}
 \tr(B_0+XB_1+YB_2)=0,
\end{equation}
is the zero triple.  In upper-triangular coordinates this is a
$121\times108$ homogeneous linear system; denote its coefficient matrix by
$C(Y)$.

The checker selects a fixed $108\times108$ row minor $M(Y)$ of $C(Y)$.  All
selected row indices are listed in the code.  For the rational center $Q$, a
rational approximate inverse gives the exact Neumann certificate
\[
 \lVert I-T_M M(Q)\rVert_\infty<2.075\cdot10^{-6},
 \qquad \lVert M(Q)^{-1}\rVert_\infty<3146.
\]
Only the terms $B_2Yv$, $B_0Yv$, and the $YB_2$ trace term vary with $Y$.
Since the largest $\ell^1$ norm among the columns of $K_0$ is $544$,
\[
 \lVert M(Y)-M(Q)\rVert_\infty
 \le c\normmax{Y-Q},\qquad
 c=\max\left\{\frac{8\cdot544}{42},8^2\right\}
 =\frac{2176}{21}<104.
\]
The exact certificate finally verifies
\[
 \lVert M(Q)^{-1}\rVert_\infty\,
 \lVert M(Y)-M(Q)\rVert_\infty
 <9.824\cdot10^{-7}<1.
\]
Therefore $M(Y)$ is invertible.  Hence $C(Y)$ has full column rank, and
Theorem 2.6(3) proves that $(X,Y)$ is matrix extreme.

\section{Why the point is not free extreme}

The source paper's Arveson equation, Theorem 2.6(1), has $gn=2\cdot8=16$
real unknowns.  Its codomain has dimension
\[
 d\,\dim\ker L_A(X,Y)=3\cdot5=15.
\]
It therefore has a nonzero solution by rank-nullity.  Thus $(X,Y)$ is not
Arveson extreme.  A matrix extreme point is irreducible, and the source
paper's Proposition 2.4 and Corollary 2.5 identify free extreme points with
irreducible Arveson extreme points.  Hence our matrix extreme point is not
free extreme.  This completes the proof of the theorem.

\section{What is exact, and what the computation does}

No semidefinite or rank conclusion is inferred from a floating-point
tolerance.  The checker performs the following proof steps with Python
integers and \texttt{Fraction} objects:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item verifies $ZK=0$, $\operatorname{rank}Z=3$, and all displayed rational
data;
\item constructs the 36-dimensional Lyapunov matrix and the
$121\times108$ matrix-extreme system from their definitions;
\item uses floating point only to propose rational approximate inverses;
\item recomputes both inverse residuals exactly and proves the stated
Neumann bounds;
\item proves the contraction and rank-stability inequalities by exact
rational comparison.
\end{enumerate}
The two approximate inverse numerators are fingerprinted in the output, but
their correctness is not assumed: every run rebuilds and checks them.

\section{Search scope, novelty, and review status}

The January 24, 2024 author manuscript included here still states that the
$g=2$ case is unknown and makes the wild-disc conjecture.  A bounded search
on 13 August 2026 for the arXiv identifier, ``wild disc'', ``non-free matrix
extreme'', and later work citing the paper found no subsequent exact
two-variable example.  This is evidence, not a substitute for a specialist
citation-database search.

The result should therefore be treated as a \emph{candidate full solution,
likely valid, novelty cautious}.  Human review should prioritize (i) the
translation from Theorem 2.6 to equations~\eqref{eq:m1}--\eqref{eq:trace},
(ii) the max-norm perturbation constant $2176/21$, and (iii) an independent
run of the exact checker.  The safety margins are large: the final
rank-stability product is below $10^{-6}$.
