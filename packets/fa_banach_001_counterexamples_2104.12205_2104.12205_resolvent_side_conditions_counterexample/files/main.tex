\begin{abstract}
Arora and Glück ask whether a one-sided rank-one estimate for a resolvent at
one real point propagates to every real point of the resolvent set when their
general domain-smoothing exponents are used.  We give a negative answer to
both the lower- and upper-estimate versions.  On three copies of
$L^2(\T)$, a positive cyclic operator transfers two components unchanged and
applies Poisson smoothing on the third transition.  Its cube, and the cube of
its adjoint, have the required smoothing, but moving the resolvent parameter
to the forbidden side exposes a negative identity channel.  Indicator
functions of shrinking arcs show that this channel cannot be bounded by the
prescribed rank-one operator.  The example satisfies the source hypotheses
with $m_1=m_2=3$.
\end{abstract}

\section{The source question}

Let $A$ be a closed, densely defined real operator on a complex Banach
lattice $E$.  The source fixes $u\in E_+$ and a strictly positive
$\varphi\in E'_+$, and assumes that, for some nonnegative integers $m_1,m_2$,
\[
 \dom(A^{m_1})\subseteq E_u,
 \qquad
 \dom((A')^{m_2})\subseteq (E')_\varphi.                 \tag{1}
\]
It also imposes a positive spectral hypothesis at a real eigenvalue.
Writing $P=u\otimes\varphi$ and
$\R(\mu,A)=(\mu-A)^{-1}$, Theorem~4.5 proves
\begin{align*}
 \R(\mu_0,A)\succeq-P, \mu\leq\mu_0
     &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \R(\mu,A)\succeq-P,\\
 \R(\mu_0,A)\preceq P, \mu\geq\mu_0
     &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \R(\mu,A)\preceq P.
                                                               \tag{2}
\end{align*}
Here $S\succeq-P$ means $S\geq-cP$ for some $c>0$, and similarly
for $\preceq$.  Proposition~4.7 removes the restrictions on $\mu$ when
$m_1=m_2=1$.  The source asks whether they can also be dropped for general
$m_1,m_2$ \cite[p.~15]{AG}.

\section*{Idea of the proof}

The source assumptions only require eventual smoothing by powers of the
operator and its adjoint.  We therefore place two unsmoothed identity
transfers before one smoothing step in a three-phase cycle.  Cubing the cycle
gives Poisson smoothing in every phase, so (1) holds with $m_1=m_2=3$.
At the initial resolvent point the cycle itself is positive.  But the exact
resolvent identity on the other side begins with $Q-\delta Q^2$; the
$-\delta Q^2$ term transports one component unchanged into a phase where the
$Q$ term vanishes.  A rank-one integral operator sees an indicator of a small
arc only through its small measure, whereas this negative identity channel
retains pointwise size one.  This mismatch violates every possible rank-one
lower bound.  Changing $A$ to $-A$ gives the upper-bound failure.

\section{The delayed-smoothing operator}

Give $\T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ normalized Lebesgue measure.  Fix
$r\in(0,1)$, for example $r=1/2$, and let $K$ be convolution on $L^2(\T)$
with the Poisson kernel
\[
 p_r(x)=\frac{1-r^2}{1-2r\cos(2\pi x)+r^2},
 \qquad \widehat{p_r}(n)=r^{|n|}.                            \tag{3}
\]
Thus $K$ is positive, self-adjoint, injective, has dense range, satisfies
$K\one=\one$, and has no other fixed vectors.  Since $p_r\in L^2(\T)$,
Young's inequality gives $K:L^2(\T)\to L^\infty(\T)$ boundedly.

Set
\[
 E=L^2(\T)^3,
 \qquad
 \varphi(f)=\frac13\sum_{j=0}^2\int_\T f_j,
 \qquad u=(\one,\one,\one),
\]
and use the coordinatewise lattice order.  Then $\varphi$ is strictly
positive and $E_u=L^\infty(\T)^3$ (as a set).  Define
\[
 Q(f_0,f_1,f_2)=(f_1,f_2,Kf_0).                             \tag{4}
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Q}
$Q$ is a bounded positive real operator, is injective, and has dense range.
Moreover,
\[
 Q^3=\operatorname{diag}(K,K,K),
 \qquad
 Q'(g_0,g_1,g_2)=(Kg_2,g_0,g_1),
 \qquad
 (Q')^3=\operatorname{diag}(K,K,K).                         \tag{5}
\]
The fixed spaces of both $Q$ and $Q'$ consist precisely of the global
constant triples $(c\one,c\one,c\one)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Positivity and (5) follow directly from (4).  Injectivity follows from that of
$K$.  The range is $L^2(\T)\times L^2(\T)\times\ran K$, which is dense
because $K$ is self-adjoint and injective.  If $Qf=f$, the first two
coordinates give $f_0=f_1=f_2$, and the last gives $Kf_0=f_0$; (3) then makes
$f_0$ constant.  The same calculation with the displayed formula for $Q'$
proves the adjoint assertion.
\end{proof}

Because $Q$ is injective with dense range, its inverse is a closed densely
defined operator.  Put
\[
 A=I-Q^{-1},\qquad \dom A=\ran Q.                           \tag{6}
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hyp}
$A$ satisfies all domination and spectral hypotheses of Setting~1.1 in the
source with $m_1=m_2=3$, $u$ and $\varphi$ as above, and $\lambda_0=0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$A$ is closed, densely defined, and real.  If $C$ is a closed operator, then
induction gives
\[
 \dom((I-C)^k)=\dom(C^k)                                   \tag{7}
\]
for every positive integer $k$: once $x\in\dom C$, membership of
$(I-C)x$ in $\dom(C^{k-1})$ is equivalent, by linearity and induction, to
membership of $Cx$ there.  With $C=Q^{-1}$, (5)--(7) yield
\[
 \dom(A^3)=\dom(Q^{-3})=\ran(Q^3)
 \subseteq L^\infty(\T)^3=E_u.                             \tag{8}
\]
The standard inverse-adjoint identity gives
$(Q^{-1})'=(Q')^{-1}$, and the same argument using $(Q')^3$ proves
\[
 \dom((A')^3)\subseteq L^\infty(\T)^3=(E')_\varphi.        \tag{9}
\]
Finally, $Af=0$ is equivalent to $Qf=f$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:Q} makes zero a
geometrically simple eigenvalue with eigenvector $u$.  Its adjoint fixed-space
statement gives the required eigenfunctional, represented by the constant
one and hence equal to $\varphi$ up to normalization.  Thus the required
strong comparisons with $u$ and $\varphi$ hold as equalities.
\end{proof}

\section{Failure of the lower estimate}

Let $P=u\otimes\varphi$.  Since
\[
 1-A=Q^{-1},\qquad \R(1,A)=Q\geq0\geq-P,                  \tag{10}
\]
the premise of the lower-estimate question holds at $\mu_0=1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:failure}
For every $\delta>0$, the number $1+\delta$ belongs to $\rhoA(A)$, but
\[
 \R(1+\delta,A)\not\succeq-P.                             \tag{11}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial identity
\[
 (I+\delta Q)(I-\delta Q+\delta^2Q^2)=I+\delta^3Q^3       \tag{12}
\]
and (5) show that $I+\delta Q$ is invertible, because
$I+\delta^3K$ is invertible.  From (6) and (12),
\[
 \R(1+\delta,A)
  =Q(I+\delta Q)^{-1}
  =(Q-\delta Q^2+\delta^2Q^3)(I+\delta^3Q^3)^{-1}.         \tag{13}
\]
Write $H=(I+\delta^3K)^{-1}$.  For $h\in L^2(\T)$, (13) gives
\[
 \R(1+\delta,A)(0,0,h)
   =(-\delta Hh,\ Hh,\ \delta^2KHh).                       \tag{14}
\]

The operator
\[
 M=\delta^3K(I+\delta^3K)^{-1}
\]
is convolution with a bounded continuous kernel: its Fourier coefficients are
\[
 \frac{\delta^3 r^{|n|}}{1+\delta^3r^{|n|}},
\]
which are absolutely summable.  Let $C$ be the supremum norm of this kernel.
Since $H=I-M$, for every measurable arc $B\subset\T$ and almost every $x\in B$,
\[
 H\one_B(x)\geq 1-C|B|.                                   \tag{15}
\]

Suppose that $\R(1+\delta,A)\geq-cP$ for some $c>0$.  Apply this operator
inequality to the positive vector $(0,0,\one_B)$.  Its $P$-image is the global
constant $|B|/3$, whereas (14)--(15) show, whenever $C|B|\leq1/2$, that the
first component of its resolvent image is at most $-\delta/2$ on $B$.  The
assumed lower bound would force
\[
 -\frac{\delta}{2}\geq-\frac{c|B|}{3}.
\]
Choosing a positive-length arc with
$|B|<\min\{1/(2C),3\delta/(2c)\}$ contradicts this inequality.  No $c$ exists,
which proves (11).
\end{proof}

\section{Answer to both parts}

\begin{theorem}[negative answer to the source question]\label{thm:main}
In the generality of Setting~1.1 of Arora--Glück, neither the condition
$\mu\leq\mu_0$ in Theorem~4.5(a) nor the condition $\mu\geq\mu_0$ in
Theorem~4.5(b) can be dropped.  This already fails with
$m_1=m_2=3$ on the Hilbert lattice $L^2(\T)^3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Lemmas~\ref{lem:hyp} and \ref{lem:failure}, together with (10), give the
counterexample to part~(a): the lower estimate holds at $\mu_0=1$ but fails
at every $\mu=1+\delta>\mu_0$.

For part~(b), replace $A$ by $B=-A$.  The domination and spectral hypotheses
are unchanged.  At $\nu_0=-1$,
\[
 \R(-1,B)=-\R(1,A)=-Q\leq0\leq P,
\]
so the upper estimate holds.  At $\nu=-1-\delta<\nu_0$,
\[
 \R(-1-\delta,B)=-\R(1+\delta,A).
\]
If this were bounded above by a positive multiple of $P$, then
$\R(1+\delta,A)$ would be bounded below by a negative multiple of $P$,
contradicting Lemma~\ref{lem:failure}.
\end{proof}

\section{Verification, limitations, and novelty}

The accompanying script discretizes $\T$ by cyclic grids of sizes
$12,24,48,96,192$.  It verifies (5) and (13) to machine precision and computes
the least constants in the finite-dimensional inequalities $S\geq-cP$.
They are approximately
\[
 17.473,\ 35.473,\ 71.473,\ 143.473,\ 287.473,
\]
and their ratio to grid size tends to $1.5$.  This is consistent with, but is
not a substitute for, the shrinking-arc proof.

The construction is an abstract Banach-lattice operator, not a differential
operator.  That is enough to answer the source's question in its stated
general setting, but it does not show that the side restrictions are necessary
for any narrower elliptic or semigroup class.  It also does not determine the
minimal smoothing exponents at which failure can first occur.

On August 17, 2026, the run's four lightweight indexes were searched by the
arXiv id, exact title, and core terms.  Bounded web searches used the exact
question, theorem terminology, title, authors, and variants of one-sided
rank-one resolvent domination.  They found the source and related later work
on eventual domination and individual maximum principles, but no explicit
resolution or this construction.  This is a bounded novelty check, not an
exhaustive priority claim.

The recommended human-review focus is (7)--(9) and the conversion of
(14)--(15) into failure of operator order.  The remaining phase algebra and
the sign reversal are direct.
